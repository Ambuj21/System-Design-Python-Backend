Design a Video Streaming Platform (Netflix-like)

A video-streaming system design question isn't "how do I serve big files fast." It's three stacked engineering problems: turn one master file into a ladder of independently-switchable renditions, let a client pick a rung every few seconds without ever running dry, and make a CDN built for immutable objects survive an audience that all wants the same still-being-produced byte at the same instant.

01 Why "store it in S3, put a CDN in front" fails the interview

Ask a mid-level engineer to design Netflix and you'll usually get: store the video files in object storage, put a CDN in front of them, maybe mention "a few different qualities for different phones." That answer survives about ninety seconds of follow-up before it collapses, because it skips the three hardest decisions in the system: how one master file becomes dozens of independently-switchable streams, how a player decides which one to request every few seconds without ever running dry, and what happens to a CDN's caching model — the one from Lesson 21 — when a live audience of millions wants the exact same byte at the exact same instant, before the origin has even finished producing it.

"A few different qualities" is doing an enormous amount of unexamined work. It isn't resizing a video file, and it isn't re-encoding a range-request-served MP4 into a handful of static alternates a client seeks into. It's a segmented, keyframe-aligned ladder — HLS or DASH — built specifically so a player can switch bitrates mid-playback without restarting the decoder. Get the segment boundaries wrong (a keyframe that doesn't land exactly where a segment starts) and bitrate switching breaks silently: no error, just a player permanently stuck on whichever rung it happened to start on.

The hands-on section below runs the actual decision logic three real video players approximate — buffer-based versus throughput-based bitrate selection — against a synthetic network cliff, with real executed numbers. The result inverts the naive intuition: the algorithm with more information available to it is not automatically the one that avoids the outage.

02 The ladder and the grid

A transcoding pipeline builds this grid before a single viewer presses play. Adaptive bitrate streaming (ABR) is entirely about which cell in the grid gets requested next.

The ladder used throughout this lesson has six rungs — 400 / 800 / 1200 / 2500 / 4500 / 8000 kbps, roughly 240p through 1080p — and every rendition is cut into 4-second segments at the exact same wall-clock timestamps. That alignment is the entire trick: segment 12 of the 400kbps stream and segment 12 of the 8000kbps stream cover the identical four seconds of the movie and both start on a keyframe (an IDR frame — a frame that decodes with no reference to any frame before it). A player switching rungs is just requesting a different row of the same column. No re-encoding, no decoder restart, no visible seam if it's done right.

That grid — rows you can jump between, columns fixed by keyframe alignment — is the entire mechanism the console below makes interactive, with three different algorithms deciding which row to pick each column.

Buffer occupancy is ground truth the instant you read it. A throughput estimate is a bet that the next four seconds look like the last four — and a sudden cliff is precisely the case where that bet loses.

04 How this shows up in systems you already use

  • Netflix per-title (and per-shot) encoding. Netflix's "dynamic optimizer" builds a custom bitrate ladder per title against a VMAF perceptual-quality curve instead of using one fixed ladder for every piece of content — a static talking-head scene needs a fraction of the bits a high-motion action scene needs for the same visible quality. Open Connect, their own CDN racked directly inside ISPs, is the delivery half of this system — the same appliance covered in Lesson 21.
  • YouTube / Google. DASH delivery with VP9 and AV1 tiers alongside plain H.264, generated across a compute cluster at upload time — older or cheaper devices only decode H.264, so several codec families ship side by side in the same ladder. Encoding at YouTube's upload volume is cited repeatedly by Google engineers as one of the largest recurring compute costs in the company, which is why AV1 hardware decode support on client devices has direct bottom-line value at their scale.
  • Twitch's 2023 transcoding change. Twitch removed automatic transcoding (all non-source renditions) for most smaller streamers — a real, public case of the compute economics in this lesson actually changing a product, not a hypothetical. Live transcoding at scale means paying GPU/CPU cost continuously for the lifetime of every stream, whether or not a given rendition is ever watched.
  • HLS vs. MPEG-DASH, converging on CMAF. Apple's HLS shipped with roughly 6-second MPEG-TS segments in 2009; DASH is the non-Apple ISO standard. Both have since converged on CMAF (Common Media Application Format) fMP4 segments, so one encode + one set of segment files can serve both HLS and DASH players instead of doubling storage and origin egress.
  • DRM at scale via CENC. Widevine (Google/Chrome/Android), PlayReady (Microsoft), and FairPlay (Apple) are three separate DRM systems every major streamer supports simultaneously. Common Encryption (CENC, ISO/IEC 23001-7) lets one encrypted segment set serve all three — only the license-acquisition wrapper differs per platform — avoiding a 3x storage multiplier stacked on top of the bitrate ladder.
  • AWS Elemental MediaConvert / MediaLive. The managed version of this entire pipeline — ladder generation, packaging, DRM — that most companies below Netflix's scale actually buy rather than build. A legitimate staff-level answer to "what would you use instead of an in-house media team" when the business case doesn't justify one.

05 Hands-on: two ABR controllers meet the same cliff, for real

Three controllers, one synthetic bandwidth trace with a real cliff in the middle. Every number below came from actually running abr_sim.py — not the console's hand-drawn timeline above, which only illustrates the shape of the mechanism.

abr_sim.py — the three decision functions + simulation core
RUNGS  = [400, 800, 1200, 2500, 4500, 8000]          # kbps
SEG_DUR, BUFFER_CAP, BUFFER_FLOOR = 4, 30, 5          # seconds

def decide_throughput(last_tp, safety=0.85):
    """Classic throughput-based: highest rung the last EWMA estimate affords."""
    afford = last_tp * safety
    rung = 0
    for i, r in enumerate(RUNGS):
        if r <= afford:
            rung = i
    return rung

# Buffer-occupancy thresholds: seconds of buffer required before a rung unlocks.
# A simplified, fixed-threshold stand-in for BOLA's Lyapunov utility function --
# same governing idea (buffer alone decides the rung), not the literal formula.
BOLA_THRESH = [0, 10, 15, 20, 25, 29]

def decide_bola(buffer):
    rung = 0
    for i, t in enumerate(BOLA_THRESH):
        if buffer >= t:
            rung = i
    return rung

def decide_hybrid(last_tp, buffer, safety=0.85):
    """Throughput-based ramp, with a reactive buffer floor as an emergency brake."""
    rung = decide_throughput(last_tp, safety)
    while rung > 0:
        est_dl = RUNGS[rung] * SEG_DUR / max(last_tp, 1)   # kbits / kbps = seconds
        if buffer - est_dl < BUFFER_FLOOR:
            rung -= 1
        else:
            break
    return rung

# simulation core (per segment i, given this segment's real bandwidth `bw`):
seg_kbits = RUNGS[rung] * SEG_DUR
download_time = seg_kbits / bw                       # seconds
if download_time > buffer:                            # STALL
    stall_time += download_time - buffer
    buffer = 0.0
else:
    buffer -= download_time
buffer = min(buffer + SEG_DUR, BUFFER_CAP)
last_tp = 0.5 * bw + 0.5 * last_tp                     # EWMA updates AFTER
                                                        # the transfer -- the
                                                        # controller could not
                                                        # have known `bw` first.

Every run's first stall is a universal ~0.19s startup stall — buffer starts at zero for every controller, so the very first segment always downloads into an empty buffer. Subtract that constant and the cliff trace's real story is: 5 cliff-caused stalls for throughput-based, 1 for BOLA-style, 1 for hybrid.

There's a real diminishing-returns point. A floor of 12s already reaches the same 1-stall floor (the unavoidable startup stall) that 18s reaches — but 18s still gives up another ~200kbps of average bitrate for zero additional protection. Past the point where the floor comfortably covers your worst realistic cliff, a bigger safety margin is pure cost. This is exactly the average-bitrate-vs-rebuffer-time trade every real ABR controller's QoE objective is built to balance — not a knob you crank to maximum and forget.

06 The senior/staff framing

What separates a strong answer from a weak one

  • Weak: "Encode into a few resolutions, use HLS, put a CDN in front." Strong: "The ladder isn't a fixed list of resolutions — it's tuned per title against a perceptual-quality curve, because a talking-head scene needs a fraction of the bits a high-motion scene needs at the same visual quality. Segment duration is a real tuning knob too: shorter segments (1-2s, LL-HLS/LL-DASH) let a player react to a bandwidth change faster and cut live latency, at the cost of compression efficiency, request volume, and manifest size."
  • Live versus VOD isn't a footnote — it changes the hardest problem in the system. A VOD segment is immutable and cacheable forever the instant it's produced, a CDN's dream object. A live segment doesn't exist yet when the first request for it arrives, and the whole audience wants it within the same sub-second window, repeated for the entire duration of the stream — not once, during a single spike.
  • The killer follow-up: "Your live event just spiked from 200K to 3M concurrent viewers in under a minute. Walk me through what happens at your origin, your shield tier, and the client's manifest-polling loop, in that order." A strong answer separates the manifest-refresh stampede — every client re-polls the .m3u8/.mpd on a fixed interval, so the manifest object itself can stampede even with segments correctly shielded — from the segment stampede. Both need independent single-flight protection; shielding one and not the other still takes the origin down.
  • Know the actual trade axis, not just "some algorithm decides": throughput-based reacts fast but is provably foolable by any bandwidth change faster than its estimator converges; pure buffer-occupancy approaches are immune to that specific failure but oscillate at a hard threshold boundary unless the cutoff is replaced with a smoothed utility function. Production players (Shaka, ExoPlayer, hls.js) ship hybrids for exactly this reason — and the "right" algorithm is the one that minimizes a QoE objective (average bitrate, minus a rebuffer-time penalty, minus a switch penalty), not the one that avoids stalls at absolutely any cost, which is trivially solved by playing 240p forever.

08 Spaced recap

Resurfacing: Lesson 21 — CDNs

Everything in this lesson's console sits on top of Lesson 21's origin-shielding mechanism, just pushed to its limit. A VOD segment is close to the easiest object a CDN ever caches — immutable, content-addressed by its rendition and sequence number, cacheable with a near-infinite TTL the instant it's produced. Live video is the opposite extreme: the object doesn't exist yet when the first request for it arrives, every edge in the footprint wants it within the same sub-second window, over and over for the life of the stream, and the manifest itself becomes a second, independent stampede surface. If "single-flight request coalescing" didn't fully click as more than a nice-to-have back in Lesson 21, a live sports stream at three million concurrent viewers is the scenario that makes it non-optional.
