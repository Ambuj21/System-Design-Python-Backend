Microservices vs Monolith

The real interview question isn't "which is better" — it's whether you can name the guarantee you lose the instant you split one process into two, and reach for the pattern (the saga) engineers built to rebuild it by hand.

01 Why this is an interview trap

Ask a mid-level engineer "when should you use microservices" and you'll usually get: "when you need to scale, or when the team gets too big for one codebase." That's not wrong. It's also the easy 80% of the answer — the part every architecture blog post already covers. It skips the part that actually separates candidates: microservices don't just change how you deploy code, they change the transaction model of your entire system.

A monolith talking to one relational database gets atomicity for free. BEGIN; UPDATE orders SET status='paid'; UPDATE inventory SET qty = qty - 1; COMMIT; — either both rows change or neither does, guaranteed by the database's write-ahead log. The instant you split Orders and Inventory into two services with two databases, that guarantee is gone. There is no longer a single log that can make both writes atomic across a network boundary. You've re-created, by hand, in application code, a problem the RDBMS used to solve invisibly.

That's usually where a "microservices vs monolith" interview question actually goes after the first minute — not "which is better" but "you just told me you'd split checkout into six services — walk me through what happens when step four fails, and who is responsible for undoing steps one through three." The console in §3 is that exact question, animated.

02 Two ways to cut the same system

There are exactly two axes engineers reach for when drawing service boundaries, and only one of them survives contact with production.

The term comes from Eric Evans' 2003 domain-driven-design work: a bounded context is the boundary inside which one domain model — its objects, its vocabulary, its invariants — stays internally consistent. "Order" inside the Orders service means something different from "order" inside Shipping (one cares about line items and pricing, the other about a package and an address), and that's not a modeling bug to fix — it's the whole point. Each bounded context owns its own schema and database, and exposes behavior through an API or an event, never a shared table.

The heuristic that holds up under interview pressure: if completing one user-facing action needs a synchronous call chain through three services where none of them is individually useful until the others finish — the left panel above — you haven't found three bounded contexts, you've found one context physically split along the wrong seam. That's a distributed monolith: every deploy is still coupled, but now every call also pays a network round trip.

Read the two cards together. A saga's failure cost scales with how much work had already committed when things went wrong — bounded by k ≤ n, never worse. True cross-service atomicity (2PC) instead scales with how many participants you asked to agree at once, and it compounds every time you add a service, whether or not anything ever actually fails. That's the real reason sagas won at this scale — not laziness, availability math.

04 How this shows up in systems you already use

  • Amazon's internal "API mandate", recounted publicly by ex-Amazon engineer Steve Yegge, required every team circa 2002 to expose data only through service interfaces — bounded-context thinking as a forcing function, nearly a decade before "microservices" was a word anyone used.
  • Netflix's 2009–2012 migration off a single Oracle-backed monolith to what is now thousands of independently deployed services is documented extensively on the Netflix tech blog, and Netflix engineers have recounted it was triggered by a 2008 database corruption incident that stalled DVD shipping for days — the canonical "one shared failure domain took down everything" story.
  • Uber's domain decomposition — trip, rider, driver and payment as separate bounded contexts — grew to roughly 2,200 microservices by 2018 per Uber engineering's own public figures. It doubles as the other cautionary tale: split too far past your team's ability to operate it, and you pay a real coordination tax for services that didn't need to be separate.
  • Netflix Conductor and AWS Step Functions are production orchestration engines: you write the sequence of steps and their compensations as a workflow definition, and the engine durably tracks which steps have committed so a crash mid-saga doesn't lose that state — solving exactly the gap called out in §6.
  • Temporal (and its predecessor, Uber's Cadence) is a "durable execution" engine purpose-built for long-running, multi-service workflows with automatic retry and compensation — widely adopted specifically because hand-rolled saga bookkeeping is where most homegrown implementations quietly rot.
  • Two-phase commit / XA transactions — the "why not just make it a real distributed transaction" instinct — exists and is real (Java's JTA, most SQL databases speak the XA protocol). It's deliberately avoided at this scale because a coordinator has to hold locks on every participant until the slowest one responds; the availability math in §3 is exactly why.

05 Hands-on: build the saga skeleton

This is the control-flow core every real saga engine wraps with durability. Run it, then break it on purpose.

saga_demo.py
"""
Minimal saga orchestrator: forward actions + compensations, run in-process.
No network/broker involved -- this is the control-flow skeleton every real
saga engine (AWS Step Functions, Netflix Conductor, Temporal) wraps with
durable state, retries and timeouts. The skeleton itself is what matters
for the interview: understanding *why* compensation only ever walks back
over steps that actually committed.
"""

from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Callable, List


class PaymentDeclined(Exception):
    pass


@dataclass
class Step:
    name: str
    action: Callable[[dict], None]
    compensate: Callable[[dict], None]


class SagaOrchestrator:
    """Runs steps in order. On failure, compensates completed steps in
    strict reverse order. This is O(n) forward, O(k) back where k <= n
    is the number of steps that had already committed -- steps that
    never started need no compensation at all."""

    def __init__(self, steps: List[Step]):
        self.steps = steps

    def run(self, ctx: dict) -> bool:
        completed: List[Step] = []
        for step in self.steps:
            print(f"  -> {step.name:<22} executing...")
            try:
                step.action(ctx)
            except Exception as exc:
                print(f"  x  {step.name:<22} FAILED: {exc}")
                self._compensate(completed, ctx)
                return False
            else:
                print(f"  ok {step.name:<22} committed")
                completed.append(step)
        print("  == saga completed successfully ==")
        return True

    def _compensate(self, completed: List[Step], ctx: dict):
        print(f"  -- compensating {len(completed)} already-committed step(s), reverse order --")
        for step in reversed(completed):
            print(f"  <- {step.name:<22} compensating...")
            step.compensate(ctx)
        print("  == saga aborted, system returned to a consistent state ==")


# ---- domain actions -------------------------------------------------

def create_order(ctx):
    ctx["order_id"] = "ORD-4471"

def cancel_order(ctx):
    print(f"       order {ctx['order_id']} marked CANCELLED")

def reserve_inventory(ctx):
    ctx["reservation_id"] = "RES-9821"

def release_inventory(ctx):
    print(f"       reservation {ctx['reservation_id']} released back to stock")

def charge_payment(ctx):
    if ctx.get("simulate_payment_failure"):
        raise PaymentDeclined("card issuer returned insufficient_funds")
    ctx["charge_id"] = "CH-3305"

def refund_payment(ctx):
    print(f"       charge {ctx.get('charge_id')} refunded")

def schedule_shipping(ctx):
    ctx["shipment_id"] = "SHP-1120"

def cancel_shipping(ctx):
    print(f"       shipment {ctx.get('shipment_id')} cancelled")

def build_saga():
    return SagaOrchestrator([
        Step("create_order", create_order, cancel_order),
        Step("reserve_inventory", reserve_inventory, release_inventory),
        Step("charge_payment", charge_payment, refund_payment),
        Step("schedule_shipping", schedule_shipping, cancel_shipping),
    ])


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("=== RUN 1: happy path ===")
    saga = build_saga()
    ok = saga.run({"simulate_payment_failure": False})
    print(f"result: {'SUCCESS' if ok else 'ABORTED'}\n")

    print("=== RUN 2: payment declined mid-saga ===")
    saga = build_saga()
    ok = saga.run({"simulate_payment_failure": True})
    print(f"result: {'SUCCESS' if ok else 'ABORTED'}")

This is the O(n)/O(k) claim from the docstring, verified: injecting the failure at steps 1, 2, 3 and 4 in turn (by swapping in a forced exception) produced exactly 0, 1, 2 and 3 compensations — never more than what had already committed. That bounded-rollback property, not raw speed, is the saga's actual guarantee.

The exercise — reason through this before writing any code:

  1. Add a fifth step, send_confirmation_sms / cancel_confirmation_sms, and confirm compensations still fire in the correct reverse order when a later step fails.
  2. Now the harder part. Imagine SagaOrchestrator is a long-lived process, and it crashes right after charge_payment commits but before schedule_shipping starts. On restart, the in-memory completed list from this code is gone. Does the orchestrator retry charge_payment (risking a double charge), skip straight to schedule_shipping, or something else — and what would you need to add to this code to make that decision safely?

The fix: persist "step X committed" as a durable row or event, keyed by a saga id, before moving to the next step — and make every action and compensation idempotent (a dedup key, a WHERE status != 'charged' guard) so a retried step after a crash is a no-op instead of a second side effect. That durable-log-plus-idempotency pair is precisely what Temporal, Step Functions and Conductor sell as a product; it's genuinely fiddly to get right by hand, which is the real argument for reaching for one of them once you have more than two or three sagas in production.

06 The senior/staff framing

What separates a strong answer from a weak one

  • Weak: "We'd break the monolith into microservices so each team can deploy independently and it scales better." Strong: "I'd decompose along bounded contexts, not technical layers, and I'd name the guarantee I'm giving up: any workflow that used to be one local ACID transaction becomes a saga — local commits plus hand-written compensations — because true cross-service atomicity (2PC/XA) makes availability multiply across every participant instead of just adding overhead."
  • The killer follow-up: "Your saga has just committed step 3 of 4 — payment charged — and the orchestrator process crashes before calling step 4. It restarts. What happens?" The honest answer points straight at the demo code in §5: the completed list lives in one process's memory. If that process dies, the record of what already committed dies with it, and on restart the orchestrator has no way to know whether to retry charge_payment (risking a second charge) or move straight to schedule_shipping.
  • The fix worth naming: a durable saga log — persist each step's commit before advancing, keyed by a saga id — plus idempotent actions and compensations, so a retried step after a crash is a no-op rather than a duplicate side effect.
  • Know the topology trade explicitly: choreography has no single point of failure or bottleneck, but the "what happens on failure" logic is scattered across every service's own event handlers — no one file shows the whole saga. Orchestration centralizes that logic into one readable, testable workflow definition, but the orchestrator becomes a component every step now depends on.

Resurfacing lesson 17 — Message Queues & Streaming

Lesson 17 covered what actually happens inside a broker: at-least-once delivery, consumer groups, offsets, backpressure. When Payment publishes PaymentFailed in the console above, that event travels through exactly the kind of broker you profiled last lesson, which means the redelivery and ordering guarantees from lesson 17 are precisely what decide whether Inventory's compensation handler runs once, cleanly, or twice, expensively.
