CAP Theorem & PACELC

Why "pick two of three" is the answer that fails a staff interview, which real systems actually lean AP vs CP and why, and the latency-vs-consistency trade you're quietly making on every single write — partition or no partition at all.

01 Why this is an interview trap

Ask a mid-level engineer to state the CAP theorem and you'll usually get: "you can only have two of Consistency, Availability, and Partition tolerance, so you have to pick." That sentence is popular enough to sound authoritative and imprecise enough to fail a staff-level bar the moment someone pushes on it.

Here's the push: Partition tolerance isn't a feature you opt into. If your system runs on more than one machine and those machines talk over a real network — the public internet, a cross-AZ link, a switch that will eventually fail — partitions will happen. You don't get to design them away; TCP/IP doesn't offer that knob. So "pick two of three" is really only ever a live decision between C and A, and only during the specific window a partition is actually occurring. Outside that window, on a healthy network, a well-built system delivers both. Eric Brewer, who first stated this as a conjecture at PODC 2000, said exactly this himself in a 2012 follow-up paper, "CAP Twelve Years Later" — the popular framing oversimplified his own point.

The second miss, just as common: CAP's C is a very specific, strong guarantee — linearizability — not the "C" in ACID. ACID consistency is about application invariants (foreign keys, uniqueness, your account balance never going negative). CAP consistency is about whether every client, everywhere, sees operations in the same real-time order, as if there were only one copy of the data. Conflating the two is the single fastest way to sound like you're pattern-matching a buzzword instead of reasoning about a mechanism.

And the deepest miss of all: CAP is silent about the 99.99% of the time your system is not partitioned. It has nothing to say about the everyday latency-vs-consistency knob that replication forces on you regardless. That gap is exactly what PACELC — a 2010 refinement from Daniel Abadi — was built to close, and it's the part of this lesson most engineers have never even heard of.

02 Two branches, one dead phone line

Two bank branches share a ledger by phone. The line goes down mid-afternoon. A customer walks into Branch A and asks to withdraw $50. Branch A has to decide, right now, with no way to ask Branch B whether it's safe.

Notice what's not in dispute here: both branches agree on what "correct" means. The disagreement is entirely about what to do the instant they can no longer verify it together. That's the whole theorem in one scene.

The console above only demonstrates whichever quadrant you've selected. Here's what all four actually mean, side by side.

PA / EL

Cassandra · DynamoDB · Riak (default configs)

Available under a partition, fast when healthy. The classic "AP" NoSQL default — conflicts get resolved after the fact via last-write-wins, vector clocks, or CRDTs.

PC / EC

HBase · BigTable · Spanner · RDBMS w/ sync replicas

Consistency wins both times: refuses writes without a reachable quorum during a partition, and waits for synchronous confirmation from replicas even when the network is perfectly healthy.

PC / EL

Yahoo PNUTS

A per-record primary keeps writes conflict-free without a synchronous round-trip, so normal-case latency stays low even though the system is strict (unavailable on conflict) under a partition.

PA / EC

rare in production

Would mean paying full synchronous-replication latency on every healthy write, yet still letting the partitioned minority diverge anyway. Hard to justify — few if any major systems default here.

04 How this shows up in systems you already use

  • DynamoDB, Cassandra, Riak — PA/EL by default. All three let you dial toward consistency per-query (Cassandra's QUORUM/ALL consistency levels, DynamoDB's strongly-consistent reads) at a direct latency cost, but their defaults favor availability and low latency. The mechanism that makes AP survivable in practice: hinted handoff (a coordinator holds writes destined for a down node and replays them once it's back), read repair and Merkle-tree anti-entropy (periodic background comparison of replica state to reconcile silent divergence) and vector clocks / dotted version vectors to detect — not just guess at — which writes actually conflict.
  • ZooKeeper, etcd, Consul — CP, on purpose. These exist specifically to be a strongly consistent source of truth for coordination (leader election, config, service discovery), built on Raft or ZAB consensus. No write is accepted without a majority quorum acknowledging it — full stop. Kubernetes uses etcd as its entire cluster state store precisely because "occasionally wrong" is not an acceptable property for the thing that decides which pod is currently the leader.
  • Google Spanner — PC/EC, but it cheats (with hardware). Spanner wants external consistency (linearizability across the whole globe) which normally means a slow synchronous consensus round-trip on every commit. Its TrueTime API — GPS and atomic clocks in every datacenter, bounding clock uncertainty to single-digit milliseconds — lets it commit as soon as it can prove no other transaction could have raced it, without an extra network round-trip purely for ordering. It's still paying the EC tax, just a much smaller one than a naive synchronous design would.
  • MongoDB — deliberately tunable, not a single point on the map. Write concern (w:1 vs w:majority) and read concern/preference let a single cluster behave close to PC/EC for one collection and closer to PA/EL for another. This is the honest answer to "is MongoDB CP or AP" — it depends entirely on the flags the calling code chose, and a strong candidate says so instead of picking a side.
  • CDNs and DNS — AP as a design philosophy, not a compromise. TTL-bounded eventual propagation is treated as a fundamental, accepted property of the domain. Nobody expects a DNS record update or a CDN cache purge to be linearizable across the planet, and no one tries to make it so.
  • Postgres synchronous_commit=on / MySQL semi-sync replication — PC/EC in a box you already run. The primary's commit blocks until the synchronous standby acknowledges the write, on every single transaction, healthy network or not. That's the EC tax paid explicitly, in exchange for zero data loss on a failover.

05 Hands-on: measure the quorum math yourself

A quorum system with N replicas, write quorum W, and read quorum R guarantees a read always sees the latest write iff W + R > N — the write set and the read set are forced to overlap by at least one replica. That single inequality is the actual mechanism underneath every "tunable consistency" knob in Cassandra, DynamoDB, and Riak, and underneath the CP/PA choice a partition forces. Below: real code, actually run, simulating five replicas with realistic latency and a real network partition.

quorum_sim.py
import threading, time

# 5 replicas modeling a cluster split across two regions:
#   R0, R1, R2 = local region   (low latency)
#   R3, R4     = remote region  (high latency) -- this becomes the "minority" side
LATENCIES_MS = {
    "R0": 2, "R1": 3, "R2": 4,      # local region
    "R3": 85, "R4": 95,             # remote region
}

def replica_ack(name, unreachable, acked, lock, done_event, needed):
    if name in unreachable:
        return  # simulates a partitioned-off node: it never responds
    time.sleep(LATENCIES_MS[name] / 1000)
    with lock:
        acked.append(name)
        if len(acked) >= needed:
            done_event.set()

def write_with_quorum(w_required, unreachable=(), timeout=0.5):
    acked = []
    lock = threading.Lock()
    done_event = threading.Event()
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    threads = [
        threading.Thread(target=replica_ack, args=(name, unreachable, acked, lock, done_event, w_required), daemon=True)
        for name in LATENCIES_MS
    ]
    for t in threads:
        t.start()
    ok = done_event.wait(timeout)
    elapsed = time.perf_counter() - t0
    return ok, elapsed, sorted(acked)

def report(label, w_required, unreachable=()):
    ok, elapsed, acked = write_with_quorum(w_required, unreachable)
    status = "ACKED" if ok else "TIMED OUT (unavailable)"
    print(f"{label:60s} {status:24s} {elapsed*1000:7.1f}ms  acked={acked}")

print("=== healthy cluster, no partition (N=5 replicas) ===")
report("W=1  (EL: ack after the single fastest replica)", 1)
report("W=3  (majority quorum ack)", 3)
report("W=5  (EC: ack after every replica)", 5)

print()
print("=== partitioned: R3,R4 unreachable -- client on the MAJORITY side ===")
report("W=1  majority side", 1, unreachable={"R3", "R4"})
report("W=3  majority side (quorum of 3 fully reachable locally)", 3, unreachable={"R3", "R4"})
report("W=5  majority side (needs all 5 -> impossible)", 5, unreachable={"R3", "R4"})

print()
print("=== partitioned: R0,R1,R2 unreachable -- client on the MINORITY side ===")
report("W=1  minority side", 1, unreachable={"R0", "R1", "R2"})
report("W=3  minority side (needs 3, only 2 reachable -> unavailable)", 3, unreachable={"R0", "R1", "R2"})

Read that last block carefully. Same code, same quorum requirement (W=3), same instant in time — one side of the partition acks in 4.4ms, the other side times out completely after half a second. That's CAP made literal: the partition didn't force a single global choice, it forced each side to independently discover whether it still has a majority. The majority side barely notices anything happened. The minority side becomes unavailable for exactly the writes that need a quorum it can't reach — which is W=3's entire point: it refuses to let a lone, cut-off replica silently drift.

The exercise — work it out before you touch the code:

# Extend LATENCIES_MS to 7 replicas (R0..R6). You want a quorum
# configuration where:
#   (a) reads and writes are guaranteed to overlap (W + R > N), and
#   (b) the cluster keeps accepting BOTH reads and writes even after
#       any 3 replicas fail or partition away simultaneously.
#
# 1. What's the minimum W (and matching R) that satisfies both
#    constraints? (Hint: "tolerates f simultaneous failures for writes"
#    means W <= N - f must still hold.)
# 2. With that W, R -- run write_with_quorum() marking exactly 3 replicas
#    unreachable. Does it ACK or TIME OUT? Now mark 4 unreachable --
#    which constraint breaks first, (a) or (b)?
# 3. This is the actual math behind "how many AZs / regions do I need"
#    conversations in a real design interview -- N=5, W=R=3 tolerates 2
#    simultaneous failures; N=7, W=R=4 tolerates 3. More replicas buys
#    more fault tolerance at the cost of paying W's latency against a
#    slower node more often. Where's the point of diminishing returns?

Answer to check yourself against: N=7 needs W=4 (and R=4 to keep W+R=8>7) to guarantee overlap while tolerating f=3 simultaneous failures — because W ≤ N - f means 4 ≤ 7-3, exactly on the boundary. Knock out a 4th replica and the write can no longer reach quorum at all: it times out, correctly, rather than committing on a minority that could conflict with an unreachable majority.

06 The senior/staff framing

What separates a strong answer from a weak one

  • Weak: "CAP theorem means you can only have two of Consistency, Availability, and Partition tolerance, so most systems pick AP or CP." Strong: "Partition tolerance isn't optional on a real network, so the live decision is C-vs-A, and only during a partition — which for most services is rare, measured in minutes per year. The trade that actually runs on every single write, every day, and directly sets my p99 latency floor is PACELC's else-clause: EL vs EC. I'd ask what the SLA actually requires before defaulting to a global CAP letter, and I'd reach for a system with tunable per-operation consistency rather than a single fixed point on the map."
  • The killer follow-up: "Your service uses Cassandra with QUORUM reads and writes — effectively W+R>N, so CP for that path. A cross-region link drops for 90 seconds. What happens to reads AND writes on the minority side during those 90 seconds, and what has to happen to reconcile state once the link comes back?" The minority side can't reach a quorum locally, so both reads and writes on that path fail fast (or time out) for the duration — it is genuinely unavailable, not just slow. Once healed: hinted handoff on the majority side replays the writes it was holding for the down replicas, and read repair plus Merkle-tree comparison reconcile any values that diverged in the meantime, using timestamps or version vectors to pick a winner.
  • The nuance worth naming unprompted: PACELC isn't a separate theorem bolted onto CAP — it's the same underlying question (favor correctness or favor responsiveness when you can't have both) asked twice: once for the rare case (a partition) and once for the constant case (normal operation). A system's full classification — PA/EL, PC/EC, PC/EL, or the rarely-chosen PA/EC — is genuinely four independent letters, and a candidate who can place a named real system on that grid, with the mechanism behind it, is operating a level above one who can only recite "eventually consistent."
  • Know the actual mechanisms, not just the labels: hinted handoff and read repair (how AP systems survive without silently corrupting data forever), the W+R>N quorum-overlap inequality (why tunable consistency levels work at all), and what "linearizable" precisely means versus the weaker models (sequential, causal, eventual) a system might actually be offering under a friendlier name.

08 Spaced recap

Resurfacing lesson 14 — Database Scaling: Replication & Sharding

Lesson 14 covered how replication and sharding physically distribute data — leader/follower replication, replication lag, and a keyspace split across shards so no single machine holds all of it. Today's lesson is the theory that explains why that distribution has consequences: the instant you have more than one copy of anything, you've created exactly the conditions CAP and PACELC describe. Replication lag is the stale-read window from the EL case in the console above, spelled out with a different name. A sharded system's per-shard leader election refusing writes without a reachable quorum is the PC mechanism, applied per shard instead of to the whole cluster. If lesson 14 answered "how do we spread data across machines," this lesson answers "and what do we now have to give up, and when, because we did."
