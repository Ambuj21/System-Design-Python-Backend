Design a URL Shortener

The system design question every candidate has already rehearsed — which is exactly why it's a filter, not a warm-up. The whole interview lives in two decisions: how you mint a short code, and how you keep a 100:1 read-heavy redirect path fast.

01 Why "just base62 the row id" isn't the answer

Ask a candidate to design a URL shortener and most reach for the same sentence in the first ninety seconds: "I'll auto-increment an id and base62-encode it." That sentence isn't wrong — it's just not an answer to a systems question yet. It's a data-structure trick standing in for a design. A staff-level interviewer hears it and immediately asks three follow-ups: what happens when two app servers try to increment at once, what happens when someone scrapes /1, /2, /3… and reconstructs every link you've ever shortened, and what happens to your database when the code your cache just evicted belongs to a link that's currently going viral. None of those are edge cases — they're the actual design.

Run the interview in order, out loud, the way it's scored:

functional requirements
  • shorten(long_url, [custom_alias], [expiry]) → short_code
  • redirect(short_code) → 301/302 to long_url
  • usually asked as a stretch goal: per-link click analytics, without slowing down the redirect itself
non-functional requirements — this is where the interview is actually scored
  • Read:write skew is extreme — a link is written once and clicked anywhere from a handful to millions of times. Assume roughly 100:1 unless told otherwise, and say so out loud; it's the number that justifies caching later.
  • Redirect latency is on the critical path of a page navigation — a user is staring at a spinner. Tens of milliseconds server-side is the budget, not hundreds.
  • Uniqueness is non-negotiable; a collision silently sends someone to the wrong page. Availability matters more than strict consistency for reads — a redirect that's a few seconds stale is invisible; a redirect service that's down is an outage.
capacity estimate (back-of-envelope, state your assumptions out loud)

Assume 500M new short links per month and a 100:1 read:write ratio.

Write QPS ≈ 500,000,000 / (30 × 86,400) ≈ ~193/s average. Read QPS ≈ 100× that ≈ ~19,300/s average, and a viral spike can push a single hour — or a single key — far past that peak.

Storage over 5 years: 500M/month × 60 months = 30 billion records. At roughly 500 bytes/record (long URL + code + metadata) that's ~15 TB of metadata before replication.

Code-space check: you need a namespace ≥ 30 billion. 626 ≈ 56.8 billion — technically enough, with almost no headroom. 627 ≈ 3.52 trillion gives you two orders of magnitude of slack and room for a non-sequential (harder to guess) scheme. Most production systems land on 7 characters for exactly that reason.

Every number above is ordinary back-of-envelope math — the deep dive below is about the two decisions those numbers force: how the code gets minted, and how the read path survives 19,300+ requests a second without hammering a database on every single one.

02 A claim-check counter with two very different lines

A URL shortener is a coat check. You hand over something bulky (a long URL) and get back a short, unique ticket (the code). The interesting part isn't the ticket — it's that the write line is short and calm, and the read line is enormous and mostly asking for the same few coats, over and over.

Two components, two very different design pressures: the ID generator is invoked rarely and must be correct and unique; the cache is invoked constantly and must be fast, because it's standing directly between a user's click and a page load.

Read the redirect card again. "No coalescing" isn't just slower — at real fan-out (a viral link can mean thousands of concurrent misses, not four) it's a self-inflicted denial-of-service against your own database, from a system that was supposed to be a thin lookup table. That's the gap between a mid-level and a staff-level answer to this question.

04 How real systems actually build this

  • Twitter's Snowflake generates 64-bit, roughly time-sortable IDs from (timestamp, worker id, sequence) with zero coordination between servers and zero database round trips — the logical endpoint of the "range allocation" idea in the console above: pay the coordination cost once, at deploy time, by fixing a worker id, instead of on every request.
  • TinyURL's original write-up popularized the Key Generation Service (KGS): pre-generate random base62 keys offline into an "unused" table, and have app servers simply claim keys from it. The hot path never computes a hash or checks for a collision at request time — the collision-avoidance work has already been done, asynchronously, before the request ever arrives.
  • bit.ly-style services accept enumerable codes on purpose. A sequential, base62-encoded row id is the simplest possible design, and it's a fine choice once you've explicitly decided the codes aren't meant to be secret — just short. The mistake isn't choosing it; it's choosing it without saying that out loud.
  • Facebook's "Scaling Memcache at Facebook" paper (2013) introduced leases specifically to stop exactly the thundering-herd pattern in the "stampede" tab above from taking down MySQL. Go's singleflight package and most modern cache-aside libraries implement the same idea: one in-flight request per key, everyone else waits on it instead of duplicating it.
  • CDNs (Cloudflare, Fastly, Akamai) cache the 301 response itself, keyed by short code, at the edge. For a genuinely viral link this is the single biggest lever on redirect latency — millions of distinct users' clicks never reach your origin at all, because the edge already has the answer.
  • Redis's single-threaded command loop is exactly why INCR makes a workable global counter in the first place: no lock contention is possible when there's only ever one thread executing commands — the same one-key idea from lesson 01, deliberately chosen here for correctness rather than avoided for throughput.

05 Hands-on: the math behind the two decisions

Three small, real experiments: base62 round-tripping, the actual collision odds behind "random hash" IDs, and how much a small LRU cache is worth against Zipfian (real-world) click traffic. All three run below — nothing here is invented.

shortener_demo.py — part 1: base62 encode/decode
import string

ALPHABET = string.digits + string.ascii_lowercase + string.ascii_uppercase  # 62 chars

def encode(num: int) -> str:
    if num == 0:
        return ALPHABET[0]
    base = len(ALPHABET)
    digits = []
    while num:
        num, rem = divmod(num, base)
        digits.append(ALPHABET[rem])
    return ''.join(reversed(digits))

def decode(code: str) -> int:
    base = len(ALPHABET)
    num = 0
    for ch in code:
        num = num * base + ALPHABET.index(ch)
    return num

for n in [1, 125, 1_000_000, 62**6 - 1, 62**6, 62**7 - 1]:
    c = encode(n)
    print(f"{n:>15,} -> {c:>8}  (len={len(c)})  round-trip ok={decode(c) == n}")
output (actual run, CPython 3.10.12)

            125 ->       21  (len=2)  round-trip ok=True

Encode/decode is O(log62 N) — each extra base62 digit multiplies your namespace by 62. Note exactly where the length steps from 6 to 7: at 626, matching the capacity estimate from section 01.

shortener_demo.py — part 2: birthday-paradox math for random hash codes
m = 62**7  # 3,521,614,606,208 — the 7-char namespace

# P(a single fresh random draw collides with an existing table of size N) ~= N/m
for existing in [1_000_000, 100_000_000, 1_000_000_000, 30_000_000_000]:
    p = existing / m
    print(f"N={existing:>14,} -> P(next insert collides)~{p:.6%}  "

# E[total collisions] over n draws into space m ~= n^2 / (2m)
for n in [10**8, 10**9, 5*10**9, 3*10**10]:

This is the number that makes "random hash" honest instead of hand-wavy: at your full 5-year scale (30B rows), roughly 1 insert in 117 collides and needs a retry with a fresh salt. That's a real, budgeted operational cost — not a theoretical footnote — and it's exactly what "range allocation" (console, tab 3) is designed to make disappear.

shortener_demo.py — part 3: LRU redirect cache vs. Zipfian click traffic
import random, bisect
from collections import OrderedDict

class LRUCache:                      # the O(1) get/put cache from lesson 25
    def __init__(self, capacity):
        self.capacity = capacity
        self.od = OrderedDict()
    def get(self, key):
        if key not in self.od:
            return None
        self.od.move_to_end(key)
        return self.od[key]
    def put(self, key, value):
        if key in self.od:
            self.od.move_to_end(key)
        self.od[key] = value
        if len(self.od) > self.capacity:
            self.od.popitem(last=False)   # evict least-recently-used

def zipf_stream(n_items, n_requests, skew=1.05, seed=42):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    weights = [1.0 / (i ** skew) for i in range(1, n_items + 1)]
    total = sum(weights)
    cum, acc = [], 0.0
    for w in weights:
        acc += w / total
        cum.append(acc)
    return [bisect.bisect_left(cum, rng.random()) for _ in range(n_requests)]

CATALOG, REQUESTS = 100_000, 500_000
stream = zipf_stream(CATALOG, REQUESTS)
CACHE_HIT_MS, DB_MISS_MS = 1.0, 12.0

for cache_size in [0, 1_000, 5_000, 20_000, 50_000]:
    cache = LRUCache(cache_size) if cache_size else None
    hits = 0
    for key in stream:
        if cache is not None and cache.get(key) is not None:
            hits += 1
        elif cache is not None:
            cache.put(key, True)
    hit_rate = hits / REQUESTS
    avg_latency = hit_rate * CACHE_HIT_MS + (1 - hit_rate) * DB_MISS_MS
    print(f"cache_size={cache_size:>6,} ({cache_size/CATALOG:>5.1%} of catalog) "
          f"-> hit_rate={hit_rate:6.2%}  avg latency={avg_latency:5.2f} ms")
output (actual run, CPython 3.10.12)
cache_size=     0 ( 0.0% of catalog) -> hit_rate= 0.00%  avg latency=12.00 ms
cache_size= 1,000 ( 1.0% of catalog) -> hit_rate=58.69%  avg latency= 5.54 ms
cache_size= 5,000 ( 5.0% of catalog) -> hit_rate=73.09%  avg latency= 3.96 ms
cache_size=20,000 (20.0% of catalog) -> hit_rate=84.37%  avg latency= 2.72 ms
cache_size=50,000 (50.0% of catalog) -> hit_rate=89.09%  avg latency= 2.20 ms

Caching just 5% of the catalog already returns 73% of requests without touching the database and cuts modeled average latency by two-thirds. That's not a property of caching in the abstract — it's a property of Zipfian (heavy-tailed) traffic, where a small set of links absorb most of the clicks. This is the exact curve that justifies the cache box in the diagram in section 02.

06 The senior/staff framing

What separates a strong answer from a weak one

  • Weak: "Auto-increment an id, base62-encode it, done." Strong: "Auto-increment is the simplest option, but it's a single point of coordination and the codes are enumerable — someone can scrape every link you've ever shortened by walking /1 through /N. If codes shouldn't be guessable, I'd either randomize with a collision check, or keep the counter but scramble it through a reversible permutation (a Feistel cipher or a fixed-coprime multiplier) so it stays O(1) and unique but stops being sequential to an outside observer."
  • The killer follow-up: "One of your links just went viral. Its cache entry expires at the exact moment ten thousand people click it in the same second. Walk me through what your database sees." Without protection: ten thousand concurrent cache misses, all for the identical row, all hitting the DB at once — a self-inflicted stampede against the one tier that doesn't horizontally scale on demand. The answer is request coalescing: a per-key lock or in-flight-request map so only the first miss actually queries the DB, and the other 9,999 await that same in-flight result (Facebook's memcache "leases," Go's singleflight).
  • The fix worth naming alongside it: proactive/probabilistic early expiration (refresh a hot key before it expires, jittered per-key) so popular entries rarely go fully cold in the first place — cheaper than reacting to the stampede after it starts.
  • Know the 301-vs-302 tradeoff, not just the caching mechanics: a 301 (permanent) lets browsers and CDNs cache the redirect themselves, which is enormous for a viral link — most repeat and edge-cached traffic never reaches your origin at all. A 302 (temporary) forces every single click through your service, which costs you throughput but buys you per-click analytics and the ability to change or kill the destination later. Naming this tradeoff unprompted is a strong signal.

Resurfacing lesson 25 — Build an LRU/LFU Cache in Python

Lesson 25 built the O(1) get/put cache — a hash map plus a doubly linked list — and asked when frequency should out-rank recency as the eviction signal. If a follow-up asks when you'd reach for LFU instead of plain LRU on this redirect cache, the answer is the same one from lesson 25 — when a small set of links (a company's root shortlink, an evergreen campaign) stay hot for weeks rather than bursting once and fading, frequency predicts the next click better than recency does.
