Scalability Fundamentals

The three ideas that open every system-design interview: how far one machine genuinely takes you before the cost curve breaks, why "add more servers" quietly requires the app to have given up its memory between requests, and the back-of-envelope math that turns a vague requirement into a number you can defend on a whiteboard.

01 Why this is where interviews go sideways

Ask a mid-level engineer how they'd scale a service and the reflex answer is "add more servers behind a load balancer." That's not wrong — it's the answer of someone who hasn't yet been burned by the two things it glosses over. First: a single machine can absorb far more growth than people assume before it needs a neighbor, and knowing roughly where that ceiling sits is a real, checkable number, not a vibe. Second: "add more servers" silently requires the application to have given up something — its memory of who you are between one request and the next. Skip either point and the interview stops being about architecture and starts being about whether you actually understood what you just said.

There's a third, quieter trap. Most system-design frameworks put back-of-envelope estimation as literally the second step, right after clarifying requirements — before a single box or arrow gets drawn. A candidate who can't turn "50 million daily active users" into a QPS figure in their head, or who loses track of whether they're at 106 or 109, loses the room in the first five minutes regardless of how good the eventual architecture is. This lesson gives you the mechanism for all three: the real limits of scaling up, the precondition for scaling out, and the arithmetic that has to come before either.

02 One desk that keeps growing, or many desks with a shared cabinet

Vertical scaling makes the one machine bigger. Horizontal scaling adds machines — but it only works if any machine can serve any request, which means the request's state can't live inside just one of them.

That single requirement — state has to live somewhere every box can reach, not inside one box's own process memory — is what people mean by a service being stateless. It doesn't mean the system holds no state anywhere; it means the process handling this request holds none of its own that the next request depends on.

04 How this shows up in systems you already use

  • Stack Overflow's famously small server footprint for most of its history — a handful of powerful, vertically-scaled SQL Server boxes plus aggressive caching — is the standing proof that vertical scaling plus good indexing goes much further than people assume for a read-heavy, well-understood workload. They didn't shard the primary database for years after companies a fraction of their traffic already had.
  • Cloud "metal" and high-memory instance families (AWS's u7in/x2iedn-class boxes, GCP's memory-optimized tier) now offer hundreds of vCPUs and tens of terabytes of RAM on a single machine — the practical vertical ceiling is far higher than most engineers guess. What doesn't scale linearly is the price: jumping from a 16-vCPU to a 32-vCPU SKU is rarely 2× the cost, it's usually 2.1–2.4×, and the largest tiers carry a real premium on top of that.
  • Kubernetes' Horizontal Pod Autoscaler literally automates the "add more boxes" half of this lesson based on CPU/memory/custom metrics — and its readiness probes implicitly assume every pod is stateless and interchangeable. An HPA scale-down that kills a pod holding unsynced in-memory state is a real, recurring production bug class, not a hypothetical.
  • Sticky sessions (classic ELB/ALB session affinity, nginx ip_hash) are the textbook anti-pattern: they make horizontal scaling appear to work by pinning a user to one box, quietly reintroducing per-box state through the back door — then bite you the moment autoscaling terminates the "wrong" instance mid-session.
  • Stateless JWTs / signed tokens exist specifically to solve the auth half of this problem: the session claim rides inside the request itself instead of living in any one server's memory, which is exactly what lets an auth-checking layer scale horizontally with zero shared-state lookup on the hot path.
  • Twitter's fan-out-on-write timeline architecture and its Redis-backed caches are the "shared filing cabinet" from the diagram, made concrete at scale: the app tier is cheap and disposable; the timeline/cache tier is where the real state — and the real scaling problem — actually lives.

05 Hands-on: write the estimator, run the numbers

The console above is this exact function, wired to sliders. Here's the code that produced the numbers below it, run for real.

capacity.py
import math


def human_bytes(n):
    """Decimal (1000-based) formatting -- the approximation interviewers
    expect at the whiteboard: 2**10 =~ 10**3, 2**20 =~ 10**6 (mega),
    2**30 =~ 10**9 (giga), 2**40 =~ 10**12 (tera). Off by ~7% at the peta
    scale -- fine for order-of-magnitude reasoning, not for a PO.
    """
    units = ["B", "KB", "MB", "GB", "TB", "PB"]
    v = float(n)
    i = 0
    while v >= 1000 and i < len(units) - 1:
        v /= 1000.0
        i += 1
    return f"{v:.2f} {units[i]}"


def capacity_report(dau, actions_per_user_per_day, avg_payload_bytes,
                     peak_ratio, retention_days, replication=3,
                     rps_per_instance=2000):
    """Back-of-envelope capacity estimate.

    rps_per_instance is a rule-of-thumb ceiling for one stateless
    synchronous JSON API instance on commodity hardware (~4-8 vCPU) --
    not a law of physics, just the number you state out loud and defend.
    """
    total_actions_per_day = dau * actions_per_user_per_day
    avg_qps = total_actions_per_day / 86400  # seconds/day
    peak_qps = avg_qps * peak_ratio
    instances_needed_at_peak = math.ceil(peak_qps / rps_per_instance)

    storage_per_day = total_actions_per_day * avg_payload_bytes
    storage_total = storage_per_day * retention_days * replication

    bandwidth_peak_bps = peak_qps * avg_payload_bytes * 8  # bits/sec

    return {
        "total_actions_per_day": total_actions_per_day,
        "avg_qps": avg_qps,
        "peak_qps": peak_qps,
        "instances_needed_at_peak": instances_needed_at_peak,
        "storage_per_day": storage_per_day,
        "storage_total": storage_total,
        "bandwidth_peak_mbps": bandwidth_peak_bps / 1_000_000,
    }


# Interview prompt: "Design a push-notification service for an 80M-DAU
# app. Each active user triggers ~5 outbound notifications and polls for
# ~15 status/read-receipt updates a day. Notifications only need to be
# replayable for 30 days."
notif = capacity_report(
    dau=80_000_000, actions_per_user_per_day=20, avg_payload_bytes=250,
    peak_ratio=6, retention_days=30, replication=3,
)

Same product, same code path, two DAU figures three orders of magnitude apart — and the second scenario never leaves "one box." That's the entire point of doing the arithmetic before designing anything: it tells you whether you're solving a distributed-systems problem or a much cheaper one.

That habit, cross-checking a whiteboard estimate against a second independent pass, is exactly what a staff interviewer is listening for when they ask "why that number, specifically?"

06 The senior/staff framing

What separates a strong answer from a weak one

  • Weak: "We'll scale horizontally — just add more servers behind a load balancer as traffic grows." Strong: "First I'd do the arithmetic: DAU times actions-per-user-per-day gives daily requests, divide by 86,400 for average QPS, multiply by a peak ratio — usually 3–6x for consumer traffic — for the number that actually matters. If that peak QPS is in the low thousands, a single well-sized instance might genuinely be the right call: fewer moving parts, no distributed-systems tax. Past that, I'd scale horizontally — which means first confirming the app tier holds no per-request state in process memory, because that's what breaks first."
  • The killer follow-up: "Your app currently keeps logged-in session data in an in-process dict — it's fast, and it works, because there's exactly one instance. Traffic is about to 10x. Walk me through what breaks the moment you add a second instance, and how you'd fix it without a full rewrite." The plausible-but-wrong fix is sticky sessions — it masks the problem instead of solving it, and still fails the moment autoscaling kills the "wrong" box or traffic gets unevenly distributed. The real fix: externalize the session to something every instance can reach — Redis, the primary database — keyed by a token the client carries, before the second instance ever goes live.
  • The nuance worth naming unprompted: horizontal scaling of stateless compute is the easy 80% of this problem. Horizontal scaling of the stateful storage tier — the database itself — is the actual hard problem: sharding, replication, and the consistency tradeoffs that come with them, which later lessons in this course cover on their own. A fleet of 50 perfectly stateless app servers still funnels every write through one database unless that's solved too.
  • Know the numbers, not just the shape of the argument: state the rps-per-instance assumption you're using out loud, state where you think the practical vertical ceiling sits, and be ready to defend both when the interviewer pushes on them — which they will.

07 Check your understanding

Before you move on

Your API runs on a single 16 vCPU / 64GB instance, holding steady at 2,000 req/s average and 6,000 req/s at peak (~70% CPU). Product tells you to expect 10× user growth over the next 12 months, with the same usage pattern — the peak ratio doesn't change.

Using the ≈2,000 req/s-per-commodity-instance rule of thumb from this lesson: how many instances do you need at peak after 10x growth, and is that a job for vertical or horizontal scaling? Now the harder part — before you add a second instance, what's the one architectural question you have to answer first, and what would you actually check in the codebase to answer it?

Answer this back to your mentor before starting the next lesson.

08 Spaced recap

Resurfacing lesson 08 — Profiling & Performance

Lesson 08 covered how to find out where a single instance's time actually goes — cProfile, flame graphs, the discipline of measuring instead of guessing. That skill is the cheapest step in capacity planning, and it comes before any of the arithmetic in this lesson: an unindexed query or an accidental O(n²) loop can eat an order of magnitude of your "2,000 req/s per instance" budget for free, silently making the QPS math above look far worse than it needs to. The honest first move when a service is "too slow" is often to profile it, not to reach for a second server — do the back-of-envelope math to know how much capacity you actually need, then use lesson 08's tools to check whether you need to buy it, or just need to fix a hot loop.
