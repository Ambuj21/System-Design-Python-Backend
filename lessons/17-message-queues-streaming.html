Message Queues & Streaming

Kafka and RabbitMQ both sit between a producer and a consumer — that's where the resemblance ends. One is an immutable log you replay; the other is a queue that forgets a message the instant it's acked. Confuse the two and the first follow-up question in a staff interview will find you.

01 Why "message queue" is an interview trap

Ask a mid-level engineer to compare Kafka and RabbitMQ and you'll usually get: "Kafka is for high-throughput streaming, RabbitMQ is for smaller task queues." That's a benchmark answer to an architecture question. It's not wrong, but it's the least interesting true thing you could say, and it dodges the part a staff interviewer is actually probing: do you know the mechanism, or just the marketing?

The real split is structural. Kafka is a partitioned, append-only commit log — consumers pull and track their own position (an offset); nothing is deleted when it's read. RabbitMQ is a broker that pushes messages to consumers and deletes each one the moment it's acknowledged. That single difference — pull-and-bookmark versus push-and-delete — is the seed every other distinction in this lesson grows out of: how replay works, how ordering works, how a crashed consumer recovers, and what "exactly-once" can and can't mean.

The second trap is "exactly-once delivery" itself. Said as a flat claim about any message system, it's almost always imprecise — the console and hands-on section below build the actual mechanism so you can say precisely where the guarantee holds and where it stops.

02 The tape and the tray

Picture Kafka as a receipt tape bolted to a wall, and RabbitMQ as a mail tray on a clerk's counter.

That's the whole architectural fork. Kafka trades "the broker deletes for you" for "you can replay anything still inside the retention window, from as many independent readers as you want." RabbitMQ trades "no replay" for "the broker actively manages routing, priority, and per-message state for you." Neither is strictly better — they're optimized for different jobs, which is exactly the framing section 06 leans on.

Sustained throughput commonly cited in the hundreds of MB/s to low-millions of msgs/sec per broker (Confluent/LinkedIn published benchmarks). End-to-end latency: single-digit ms achievable with tuned linger.ms/acks; tens of ms typical unturned. RabbitMQ, order of magnitude

classic mirrored/quorum queue, small messages, commodity hardware

Tens of thousands of msgs/sec per queue is the commonly cited ceiling; quorum queues (Raft-replicated) trade some of that for durability. Per-message latency is often lower than Kafka's for small task-queue workloads. Scaling out means adding more queues (sharding by hand), not adding partitions to one queue — that's an architectural decision, not a config flag.

04 How this shows up in systems you already use

  • LinkedIn / Uber / Netflix run Kafka as the event backbone — trip events, feed activity, and Netflix's Keystone pipeline process on the order of trillions of events per day industry-wide across such deployments, precisely because the log lets dozens of independent teams attach new consumer groups to the same stream without touching the producer or each other.
  • Debezium + Kafka Connect for CDC turns a Postgres/MySQL write-ahead log into a Kafka topic. This is the log-replay property doing real work: a new downstream consumer (a search index, a cache warmer, an analytics job) can be added later and replay from the retention window instead of needing a special one-time backfill pipeline.
  • Celery's default broker is RabbitMQ (or Redis) precisely because task queues want push dispatch, per-task ack/retry, priority, and delayed delivery — not replay. A "resize this image" task doesn't need three independent consumer groups reading it back later; it needs exactly one worker to pick it up and for the broker to redeliver if that worker dies mid-task.
  • AWS SQS vs Kinesis mirrors this exact fork. Standard SQS is broker-managed, at-least-once, deletes-on-ack (RabbitMQ's model, hosted). Kinesis is a partitioned, replayable log with shard iterators standing in for Kafka's offsets (Kafka's model, hosted). Same architectural question, same two answers, different vendor names.
  • KEDA and Kafka-lag-based autoscaling. Kubernetes' KEDA project scales consumer pod replicas directly off a Kafka consumer group's reported lag metric — the same "lag is the signal, not broker pushback" mechanism the console above demonstrates, wired straight into an autoscaler's decision loop.
  • Reactive Streams / gRPC flow control / raw TCP all implement the same credit-based backpressure idea RabbitMQ uses at the message level: a receiver advertises how much it can absorb, and the sender is mechanically prevented from exceeding it — TCP's receive window is the oldest version of this pattern in daily use.

05 Hands-on: the two mechanisms, not a mock

Neither of these needs a running broker — they are the bookkeeping algorithms the broker/consumer runtime executes, lifted out so you can watch them decide. The functions below are real, runnable Python; the traces underneath are annotated walkthroughs of exactly what those functions compute for the inputs shown, not a raw captured terminal session — every number in them follows mechanically from the code, and you can paste the functions into a REPL and check.

mq_mechanisms.py — part 1: consumer group partition assignment
def range_assign(partitions, consumers):
    """Kafka's default RangeAssignor: partitions sorted, sliced
    contiguously per consumer (sorted by member id), remainder
    given to the first N consumers in sorted order."""
    consumers = sorted(consumers)
    n = len(consumers)
    p = len(partitions)
    base, extra = divmod(p, n)
    assignment = {}
    idx = 0
    for i, c in enumerate(consumers):
        count = base + (1 if i < extra else 0)
        assignment[c] = partitions[idx:idx + count]
        idx += count
    return assignment

TOPIC_PARTITIONS = list(range(6))  # "orders" topic, 6 partitions
group = ["consumer-A", "consumer-B", "consumer-C"]
print(range_assign(TOPIC_PARTITIONS, group))

  Messages B had read but not yet committed get redelivered to C.
  That redelivery-after-failure IS the at-least-once guarantee.

=== Now scale OUT: a 4th consumer joins (partitions < consumers) ===
  consumer-A: partitions [0, 1]
  consumer-C: partitions [2, 3]
  consumer-D: partitions [4]
  consumer-E: partitions [5]
  Partition count is a hard ceiling on consumer parallelism: you
  cannot have more *active* consumers in one group than partitions.

Note what the rebalance actually resumes from: the last committed offset, not "wherever the dead consumer was." That gap between "last read" and "last committed" is precisely the window that gets reprocessed — it's the mechanical reason at-least-once is the default, not a design choice someone made casually.

mq_mechanisms.py — part 2: idempotent producer dedup (the real "exactly-once" mechanism)
class BrokerPartitionLog:
    """Exactly what a Kafka partition leader does for an idempotent
    producer: remember the last committed sequence number per
    producer_id, reject/dedup anything that isn't the next expected
    value."""
    def __init__(self):
        self.log = []
        self.last_committed_seq = {}

    def append(self, producer_id, seq, payload):
        last = self.last_committed_seq.get(producer_id, -1)
        if seq <= last:
            return ("DUPLICATE_IGNORED", None)
        if seq != last + 1:
            return ("OUT_OF_ORDER_REJECTED", None)
        self.last_committed_seq[producer_id] = seq
        offset = len(self.log)
        self.log.append(payload)
        return ("APPENDED", offset)

def send_with_retries(broker, producer_id, seq, payload, ack_gets_lost_once):
    """Producer resends on ack timeout -- exactly what happens on a real
    TCP blip or broker failover. The broker already appended the record;
    only the ACK vanished."""
    attempts = []
    for attempt in range(1, 4):
        status, offset = broker.append(producer_id, seq, payload)
        attempts.append((attempt, status, offset))
        if ack_gets_lost_once and attempt == 1:
            continue
        break
    return attempts

  attempt 1: (pid=producer-7f3a-.., seq=0) -> APPENDED @ offset 0
  attempt 2: (pid=producer-7f3a-.., seq=0) -> DUPLICATE_IGNORED

  -> exactly ONE copy landed, even though the wire carried the request twice.

=== Same producer, next real message (seq=1), then a genuine reorder attempt ===
  seq=1 -> APPENDED @ offset 1
  seq=3 (skipped 2) -> OUT_OF_ORDER_REJECTED  <- broker refuses to create a gap

  This is 'exactly-once' as it is ACTUALLY implemented: at-least-once
  delivery over the wire, plus broker-side dedup keyed on (producer_id,
  sequence). It does not mean the message was sent once -- it means it
  is committed to the log at most once, in order, per producer session.

What this buys you, precisely: exactly-once from one producer instance to one partition. Real Kafka tracks up to max.in.flight.requests.per.connection (default 5) unacked sequence numbers in a sliding window rather than just one, and requires acks=all so the dedup state itself is replicated before it's trusted. Getting exactly-once across multiple partitions, or out to a non-Kafka sink like Postgres, needs Kafka's transactional producer (producer epochs + a transaction coordinator) on top of this — idempotence alone doesn't reach that far.

Exercise: extend BrokerPartitionLog to model a "zombie producer" — a second instance of the same logical producer that reconnects and starts sending seq=0 again after a network partition healed on the old instance too. Real Kafka fences this with a monotonically increasing producer epoch: a new producer session bumps the epoch, and the broker rejects any write carrying an older epoch outright, even a perfectly in-order one. Add an epoch parameter to append() and reject writes where epoch < last_epoch_seen before you even look at the sequence number — that ordering of checks is the actual fix for split-brain producers, and it's worth predicting the failure mode before you write it.

06 The senior/staff framing

What separates a strong answer from a weak one

  • Weak: "We picked Kafka because it scales better than RabbitMQ." Strong: "We picked Kafka because we need three independent teams to replay the same order-event stream from different points in time, and because ordering only needs to hold per customer_id, which maps cleanly to a partition key. If we needed per-message priority, delayed delivery, or complex routing to different consumer types, RabbitMQ's exchange model would cost us less custom code than bolting those onto Kafka."
  • The killer follow-up: "You need exactly-once processing from a Kafka source topic all the way into a Postgres sink table. Kafka's idempotent producer only guarantees exactly-once from producer to broker — how do you get exactly-once out the other end, into a database that's never heard of a producer epoch?" The honest answer has no shortcut: either make the sink write idempotent yourself — a dedup table keyed on (partition, offset) with an upsert, so replaying the same offset twice is a no-op — or use a Kafka Connect sink connector built for exactly-once semantics, which does exactly that dedup under the hood. "Exactly-once" past Kafka's own boundary is always at-least-once delivery plus an idempotent consumer, dressed up.
  • Know the ordering guarantee precisely: Kafka guarantees order within a partition, not across a topic. Pick a partition key that puts everything that must stay ordered relative to each other (e.g. all events for one order_id) on the same partition — get the key wrong and you've silently broken the one guarantee people assume Kafka gives them.
  • Know the backpressure asymmetry cold: Kafka's producer never blocks because a consumer is slow — lag just grows, and the fix is horizontal (more consumers, up to the partition ceiling) or algorithmic (faster per-record handling). RabbitMQ's broker can and does block a publisher's socket once a queue crosses a memory/disk watermark — backpressure is enforced, not just measured.

08 Spaced recap

From Lesson 12 — Load Balancing

Lesson 12 covered how an L7 load balancer spreads requests across stateless backends — round robin, least-connections, consistent hashing — and pulls a backend out of rotation the moment its health check fails. Today's mechanisms are the same problem wearing different clothes. RabbitMQ's round-robin dispatch to competing consumers is the identical algorithm you saw balancing HTTP requests, just balancing messages instead. And a Kafka consumer-group rebalance is a health-check-driven failover: the group coordinator's missed-heartbeat detection plays the exact role a load balancer's health check plays, and reassigning partitions to a live consumer is the same move as pulling a dead backend out of rotation and routing its traffic elsewhere. The difference worth naming in an interview: an LB rebalances continuously and cheaply per-request; a Kafka rebalance is comparatively expensive and — under the eager assignors — briefly stops healthy partitions too, which is exactly why KIP-429 was worth building.
