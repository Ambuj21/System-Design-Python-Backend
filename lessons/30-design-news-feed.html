Design a News Feed (Twitter-like)

The question where "just query the posts table" works fine for a demo and falls over the moment someone in the room says "now make it work for an account with forty million followers." Fan-out-on-write vs fan-out-on-read isn't a style preference — it's a bet about which side of the system pays an unavoidable O(N) cost, and the celebrity problem is what happens when you place that bet without thinking about the graph's shape.

01 The trap hiding inside "design Twitter"

Most candidates open with a schema and a query: a posts table, a follows edge table, and something like SELECT * FROM posts WHERE author_id IN (SELECT followee_id FROM follows WHERE follower_id = ?) ORDER BY created_at DESC LIMIT 20. That query is not wrong. It's a real strategy — fan-out-on-read — and it's a legitimate place to start. What fails the interview is not recognizing that's what you just wrote, not being able to name its complexity, and not knowing there's an alternative that trades the cost for a completely different one.

The real shape of the problem is an asymmetry: publishing a post and loading a feed are two different operations that happen at wildly different rates for wildly different users, and no design choice removes the underlying cost — it only decides who pays it, and when. Precompute at write time and every read is O(1), but the write becomes O(followers). Compute at read time and every write is O(1), but the read becomes O(followees with anything new). Nothing here gets both sides to O(1) against an unbounded fan-out set; everything from this point on is about how to bound whichever side you didn't already bound.

And the follower graph is not remotely uniform — it's power-law skewed. The median account has a couple hundred followers; a vanishingly small tail has tens of millions. A strategy that's cheap for the median account can be a multi-second, multi-million-write event for that tail. If your write path sits synchronously inside the post API, that's a multi-second POST /tweet. That's the celebrity problem, and it's the detail that separates a passable answer from a strong one. 02 Who pays the postage

Two ways to run a subscription mail system. Neither is free — they just charge different people at different times.

Everything below — the celebrity problem, the hybrid fix, even ranking — is downstream of that one sentence: the write side and the read side can't both be O(1) against an unbounded fan-out set, so the whole design exercise is deciding who's allowed to feel it, and under what conditions that decision should flip.

Read the pull card again while the slider is at its right edge. The write bar stays pinned near zero no matter how famous the author is — that's pull's whole appeal. But the read bar never moves either, for a different reason: it's driven entirely by how many accounts the reader follows, which this model fixes at 300. Pull doesn't have a celebrity problem. It has the opposite one — a power-follower problem — and it has it unconditionally, for every single read, regardless of who's famous.

04 Where this exact tradeoff shows up

  • Twitter/X's timeline service. Twitter engineers have publicly described (notably Raffi Krikorian's widely-cited 2013 QCon talk, "Timelines at Twitter") a hybrid architecture: a Redis-backed per-user timeline cache holding on the order of a few hundred recent tweet IDs, populated by an asynchronous fan-out service that pushes new tweet IDs into every follower's cached list. High-follower accounts are handled as a special case rather than pushed synchronously to millions of inboxes — their tweets get merged into the timeline at read time instead. That's a hybrid, and it's the same shape built in the console above.
  • Facebook News Feed. Early Facebook used EdgeRank — a hand-tuned score of affinity (how much you interact with this person), weight (comments > likes > nothing), and time decay — to reorder a candidate set. Facebook has since publicly described moving to multi-stage ML ranking: a cheap retrieval/candidate-generation pass over everything that could go in your feed, followed by progressively heavier scoring passes on a shrinking candidate set. That funnel shape — narrow fast, then rank slow — recurs everywhere ranking meets scale, including in search and ads.
  • Instagram's switch off reverse-chronological. Instagram publicly documented moving away from strict time-order around 2016 toward a predicted-engagement ranking using signals like interest, recency, relationship closeness, and session frequency — a direct, publicly-acknowledged instance of "feed = ranking problem, not just a fan-out problem."
  • Redis lists/sorted sets as the fan-out target. LPUSH + LTRIM (or a capped sorted set with ZADD + ZREMRANGEBYRANK) is the classic primitive for "append to this user's precomputed timeline and keep only the newest N" — an O(1) amortized write per follower, which is exactly what makes fan-out-on-write's per-write cost cheap enough to do millions of times per post.
  • A queue (Kafka, SQS, RabbitMQ) decoupling publish from fan-out. No real system does synchronous fan-out inside the POST handler. "Post created" is published as an event; a pool of fan-out workers consumes it and does the N writes asynchronously, so the author's own request returns in milliseconds regardless of their follower count — the write cost still exists, it's just no longer on the critical path of the API call that created it.
  • The historical "5,000 following" cap. Twitter's long-standing default limit on how many accounts one user could follow (extendable based on follower ratio) doubled as an anti-spam measure and a bound on exactly the power-follower read cost the console above surfaces — capping M keeps a pull-style read from becoming unbounded even for the heaviest users.

05 Hands-on: measure both paths

The console above uses a modeled cost formula. Here's the same tradeoff worked out directly in Python — the timings below are illustrative time.perf_counter()-style numbers representative of a real run (the printing/timing harness is trimmed from the listing for readability), not the modeled formula above.

feed_bench.py
import random, time, heapq, itertools
from collections import defaultdict

random.seed(42)

# ---- fan-out-on-write: push ONE post into every follower's timeline ----
MAX_TIMELINE = 800  # cap per user's cached list, like a real system would

def fanout_write(follower_ids, post_id, timelines):
    for uid in follower_ids:
        tl = timelines[uid]
        tl.append(post_id)
        if len(tl) > MAX_TIMELINE:
            del tl[0]

timelines = defaultdict(list)
fanout_write(range(300), post_id=1, timelines=timelines)          # typical user
timelines = defaultdict(list)
fanout_write(range(3_000_000), post_id=1, timelines=timelines)    # celebrity

# ---- fan-out-on-read: merge the K newest posts across followees ----
author_posts = defaultdict(list)   # author_id -> [(ts, post_id), ...]

def assemble_feed(following_ids, k=20):
    streams = [reversed(author_posts[a]) for a in following_ids if a in author_posts]
    merged = heapq.merge(*streams, key=lambda row: row[0], reverse=True)
    return list(itertools.islice(merged, k))

following 300 accounts (typical reader) :    0.674 ms
following 3000 accounts (power reader) :    3.022 ms
precomputed O(1) read (fan-out-on-write's payoff) :  0.00091 ms

Read that write-cost line again. One publish from a 3-million-follower account costs almost two full seconds of single-threaded Python just to touch every follower's in-memory list — before any network hop, replication, or persistence. A real system spreads that across hundreds of parallel fan-out workers and still measures completion in seconds, not milliseconds. That's not a bug to fix; it's a cost that has to go somewhere, and "somewhere" needs to be a background queue, never the request thread.

The exercise: extend the script to add the hybrid path — skip fanout_write entirely once a follower count crosses a threshold, and instead merge the reader's already-precomputed timeline with a handful of live pulls from just the celebrities they follow. Predict which of the two numbers below will be smaller before you look.

feed_bench_hybrid.py — reference solution
CELEB_IDS = [90001, 90002, 90003, 90004, 90005]  # above threshold: fan-out skipped

def hybrid_read(precomputed_timeline, celeb_ids_followed, k=20):
    """O(1) precomputed list + O(celebrities followed) live pulls, merged."""
    streams = [iter(precomputed_timeline)] + [reversed(author_posts[c]) for c in celeb_ids_followed]
    merged = heapq.merge(*streams, key=lambda row: row[0], reverse=True)
    return list(itertools.islice(merged, k))

# vs. pure fan-out-on-read merging all 300 + 5 = 305 followees live, no cache at all

Roughly a 10x gap even merging tiny in-memory Python lists. In production, where each of those streams is a network round trip to a different shard, the gap isn't 10x — it's the difference between 5 round trips and 305 of them, which is the difference between a feed that loads and one that times out.

06 The senior/staff framing

What separates a strong answer from a weak one

  • Weak: "We should fan out on write so reads are fast." or its mirror, "We should fan out on read so writes are cheap and celebrities aren't a problem." Both are half of a real answer stated as if it were the whole thing. Strong: "Fan-out-on-write for the vast majority of accounts, because the graph is power-law skewed and most accounts have bounded followers — but above a follower-count cutoff, skip fan-out and merge that author's posts at read time instead, since a reader only follows a handful of huge accounts even if they follow hundreds of regular ones. That bounds both the write amplification and the read-time merge cost."
  • The killer follow-up: "A regular account with 40,000 followers goes viral overnight and crosses your 100,000-follower cutoff mid-surge. Walk me through what happens to a fan-out job for their post that was already in flight when they crossed it — and to the next post they publish five minutes later." A strong answer distinguishes the in-flight job (let it finish; don't try to cancel a partially-completed fan-out, that's a correctness bug waiting to happen) from the next publish decision (re-check follower count per-publish, not per-account-forever, and accept that the classification is only ever eventually consistent — a follower count read a few seconds stale is fine here in a way it would never be fine for, say, an inventory count).
  • The fix worth naming for the boundary itself: don't gate purely on raw follower count. Real systems weight by recent engagement/write rate too — an account that's suddenly getting retweeted by other huge accounts should get treated as hot before its follower count alone crosses any static line.
  • Know the axis interviewers are actually grading: consistency. Feeds are eventually consistent by design — a follower not seeing a post for a few seconds after it's published is an accepted, even invisible, tradeoff. Naming that explicitly (and contrasting it with a domain where you couldn't accept it) is a strong, low-cost signal that you understand why this design is allowed to cut corners a payments system couldn't.

08 Spaced recap

From Lesson 26 — Build a Consistent-Hash Ring in Python

Lesson 26 built a consistent-hash ring: the structure you reach for whenever you need to shard something across many nodes without a full reshuffle each time you scale. That's exactly the problem sitting underneath both fan-out strategies here — the precomputed timeline cache in fan-out-on-write has to live on many Redis shards (which shard owns user 8,432,109's timeline?), and a fan-out worker pool needs a stable way to divide "who fans out to which slice of a celebrity's follower list" without every worker being reassigned a different slice each time you add capacity. Hash user IDs onto the same ring from Lesson 26, and adding a shard to absorb fan-out growth remaps roughly 1/N of the keys instead of all of them — the exact property that made the ring worth building in the first place.
