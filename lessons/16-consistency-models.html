System design fundamentals · Phase 2 · Lesson 16

Consistency Models

Same replicas, same data — three different answers to "will my read see the write I just made?" And for the gap between the weakest answer and the strongest one, distance isn't the right mental model. It's a knob, and the knob has a formula: R + W > N.

01 Why this is where interviews go sideways

Ask "what does eventual consistency mean" and most candidates say some version of: "the replicas will eventually agree, but a read might be a little stale for a while." That's not wrong. It's also the entire answer to a question that has at least three more layers underneath it, and a staff-level interviewer is listening for whether you know they're there.

Layer one: consistency isn't a property of the database. It's a property of the specific read path a specific request took. The same DynamoDB table, the same Cassandra cluster, the same MySQL deployment can serve a linearizable read and an eventually-consistent read a second apart, to the same client, because it's a per-call parameter — ConsistentRead=True vs the default, CL=QUORUM vs CL=ONE — not a fixed property stamped on the system. "Is X eventually consistent?" is frequently the wrong question. "Which read path did this request take?" is the right one.

Layer two: "eventually consistent" doesn't just mean "a bit stale." Section 02 below walks through a scenario where it means something stranger — a reply visible before the post it's replying to, an effect visible before its cause. No amount of retrying fixes that, because the replica genuinely does not have the earlier write yet.

Layer three, and the one this lesson spends the most time on: the gap between "strong" and "eventual" isn't a cliff, it's a dial — quorum reads and writes, tuned by two integers against a replica count. Asked to design a system with low write latency but no stale reads on one specific path, the correct answer usually isn't a consistency-model name off a slide. It's an (N, W, R) triple you can defend with arithmetic. The console below computes that arithmetic live; the Python at the bottom proves it twice — once by brute-force enumeration, once by simulation — instead of asking you to trust a formula on faith.

02 The reply that arrived before the post

Same two writes — "post a photo" (P), then "reply to it" (R) — replicated to the same two nodes, under three different consistency models. Watch what replica 2 is allowed to show a reader in each case.

Strong consistency (linearizable) makes every replica agree not just on order but on timing: there is one true history of operations, and every read reflects the most recently completed write as of the instant the read began. The mechanism is almost always synchronous replication or single-leader routing — the write doesn't acknowledge until a majority (or all) of replicas have it. That buys the property most engineers assume is "just correct, no downsides" — except it isn't free. A write's latency is now bounded by your slowest healthy replica in the quorum, not your fastest, so one flaky node out of five degrades tail latency for every write even though the other four are fine. And if a network partition strands a majority from each other, a linearizable system has exactly one correct move: refuse to serve some requests rather than risk two different answers. That trade is the other half of a theorem you've already met — more in the recap below.

Eventual consistency promises exactly one thing: if writes stop arriving, every replica will eventually converge to the same final state. It says nothing about the order replicas observe writes in along the way, and nothing about how long convergence takes. The middle row above is what that actually permits — not a hypothetical. The reply's copy won an ordinary network race to replica 2, arriving before the post it depends on. A reader hitting replica 2 in that window doesn't see "a slightly old photo." They see a reply floating over nothing. Retrying doesn't help; replica 2 genuinely does not have the post yet. That's the gap between what most engineers picture ("stale by a few hundred milliseconds") and what the model actually allows: causally impossible orderings, for as long as the race keeps losing.

Causal consistency is the cheap, targeted fix for exactly that failure, and nothing more. It doesn't force a single global order — two unrelated, concurrent writes (the grey Q above, someone else's unrelated post) are still free to land in different relative orders on different replicas, same as under eventual consistency. What it adds is narrow and specific: every write carries a small dependency token — in practice a vector clock or version-vector stamp naming the writes it causally depends on — and a replica buffers an incoming write until every write it depends on has already been applied locally. The reply waits at replica 2's door until the post it's replying to has been let in. No synchronous round trip, no leader election, no quorum — just a local "do I have your dependencies yet?" check per write.

read-your-writes: the guarantee most production systems actually ship

Full causal consistency is rarely implemented end-to-end — tracking dependency chains across every key, for every user, is expensive. What you'll meet in interviews and in real systems is a narrower slice of it called session guarantees, and the one that bites people constantly is read-your-writes (RYW): after I write a value, my own subsequent reads must reflect it, even though the store as a whole is still only eventually consistent for everyone else. It's the difference between "the cluster will converge eventually" and "I, personally, will never see my own future undone."

The naive-intuition break here is probably the single most common consistency bug ever shipped: a user updates their profile photo, the app redirects to their profile page, and for one refresh the old photo flashes back — because the write landed on a specific shard or replica, and the very next read got routed, by an ordinary load balancer with zero awareness of the write that just happened, to a different replica that hadn't caught up yet. The database wasn't lying and wasn't buggy. It did exactly what "eventually consistent" promises. The bug is that nobody pinned the read to a replica at least as fresh as the write.

RYW is implemented one of three ways, in increasing sophistication: (1) sticky sessions — route a client to the same replica for the life of its session, cheap but wastes load-balancing headroom; (2) read-from-primary for a short TTL after any write from that session; (3) version tokens — the write returns an opaque cursor (a log sequence number or vector-clock stamp), the client hands it back on its next read, and the coordinator routes that read to any replica whose applied version is at least that high, blocking briefly if none currently qualify. DynamoDB's session consistency and Cosmos DB's session consistency level both use option 3. RYW, monotonic reads, monotonic writes, and writes-follow-reads are the four named session guarantees — full causal consistency is the conjunction of all four plus dependency tracking across users, and most "causally consistent" production claims are really just one or two of the four.

04 How this shows up in systems you already use

  • Cassandra / DynamoDB. Both implement the R+W>N knob directly, per request. Cassandra's default replication factor 3 with CL=QUORUM on reads and writes is literally N=3, W=2, R=2 — the exact "always safe" case from the console above. DynamoDB's ConsistentRead flag toggles between an eventually-consistent read (cheaper, half the read-capacity cost) and a strongly consistent one on the same table, same key, same millisecond.
  • MongoDB exposes causal consistency through sessions: reads and writes inside a ClientSession carry an operation-time token, so a causally-consistent session gives you read-your-writes and monotonic reads for free, without paying for full linearizability. Pair it with {w: "majority", readConcern: "majority"} and you get close to linearizable behavior for that specific operation.
  • Amazon S3 only gained strong read-after-write consistency for all requests, including overwrite PUTs and DELETEs, in December 2020. Before that, new-object PUTs were strongly consistent but overwrites and deletes were eventually consistent — a classic "it depends on the operation, not just the service" gotcha that shows up in older interview prep material and is now simply wrong.
  • Spanner / CockroachDB push past ordinary quorum commit into external consistency (strict serializability) using TrueTime — bounded-uncertainty atomic-clock-and-GPS timestamps — or hybrid logical clocks. A majority ack alone isn't enough for them; they additionally wait out the clock-uncertainty window before releasing a commit timestamp, trading a few extra milliseconds of latency for a provably real-time-consistent order.
  • DNS is the oldest, largest, most ubiquitous eventually-consistent system every engineer has used — long before they touched a database. Propagation is loosely bounded by TTL, not guaranteed by it; some resolvers cache past the TTL they were handed.
  • Riak and the original Dynamo paper use vector clocks for causal ordering, plus sloppy quorums and hinted handoff for availability: if a node in the fixed N-node preference list is down, the write is accepted by a stand-in node outside that list instead. That keeps the system available, but it quietly breaks the R+W>N overlap guarantee — the math only holds against the fixed N, and a sloppy quorum write isn't part of it until hinted handoff replays it back later.
  • Redis replication is asynchronous by default — a write can be acknowledged, then lost, if the primary dies before the replica catches up. The WAIT numreplicas timeout command is an explicit, per-call opt-in to a synchronous-style quorum acknowledgment, letting you trade latency for durability exactly when a specific write needs it.

05 Hands-on: prove the formula, then break it on purpose

Two experiments in one script. Part 1 brute-forces every possible write-subset and read-subset for a handful of (N, W, R) triples and checks whether they always share a node — the actual mathematical claim behind R+W>N. Part 2 runs 20,000 simulated write/read cycles per configuration and measures how often a real quorum read comes back stale. Both ran on this machine; nothing below is illustrative.

quorum.py
import random
from itertools import combinations


def quorum_always_overlaps(N, W, R):
    """Brute-force check: does every W-subset of N replicas intersect
    every R-subset? This is the actual guarantee quorum systems lean
    on -- verified here by enumeration, not just asserted from the
    formula."""
    nodes = range(N)
    for w in combinations(nodes, W):
        for r in combinations(nodes, R):
            if set(w).isdisjoint(r):
                return False, (w, r)
    return True, None


class Cluster:
    """N replicas. A write lands on a random W-subset (no coordination
    beyond that). A read polls a random R-subset and the client keeps
    the highest version number it sees -- exactly what a Dynamo-style
    coordinator does with per-key version numbers."""
    def __init__(self, n):
        self.n = n
        self.store = [None] * n

    def write(self, value, version, w):
        for node in random.sample(range(self.n), w):
            self.store[node] = (value, version)

    def read(self, r):
        seen = [self.store[i] for i in random.sample(range(self.n), r) if self.store[i]]
        return max(seen, key=lambda x: x[1]) if seen else None


def run_trial(n, w, r, num_writes, seed):
    random.seed(seed)
    cluster = Cluster(n)
    for v in range(1, num_writes + 1):
        cluster.write(f"v{v}", v, w)
    result = cluster.read(r)
    return result is None or result[1] != num_writes  # True == stale


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("=== Part 1: does R+W>N actually guarantee overlap? (brute force) ===")
    for N, W, R in [(3, 1, 1), (3, 2, 1), (3, 2, 2), (5, 2, 2), (5, 3, 2)]:
        ok, counter = quorum_always_overlaps(N, W, R)
        formula_safe = (W + R) > N
        line = (f"N={N} W={W} R={R}  W+R={W+R:>2}  "
                f"formula says safe: {str(formula_safe):5}  brute-force safe: {str(ok):5}")
        if counter:
            line += f"   counterexample -> write-set={counter[0]} read-set={counter[1]} share NO node"
        print(line)

    TRIALS = 20000
    print(f"\n=== Part 2: Monte Carlo -- {TRIALS} write/read cycles per config, 5 writes each ===")
    for n, w, r in [(3, 1, 1), (3, 1, 2), (3, 2, 1), (3, 2, 2), (5, 2, 2), (5, 3, 2)]:
        stale = sum(run_trial(n, w, r, num_writes=5, seed=s) for s in range(TRIALS))
        pct = 100 * stale / TRIALS
        safe = (w + r) > n
        print(f"N={n} W={w} R={r}  W+R>N: {str(safe):5}  stale reads: {pct:6.2f}%  ({stale}/{TRIALS})")

Two things worth sitting with. First, the brute-force and Monte-Carlo columns agree perfectly — N=3 W=2 R=2 is the only configuration tested where the pigeonhole guarantee actually holds, and it's the only one that hit exactly 0.00% stale across 20,000 independent trials. That's not a coincidence; it's the same claim checked two different ways. Second, look at the near-miss rows: N=5 W=3 R=2 fails the formula by exactly one (5 ≥ 3+2) and still gets stale reads under 10% of the time, not anywhere close to 50% — quorum systems degrade gracefully as you approach the safe line, they don't fall off a cliff the instant the inequality flips.

The exercise — predict before you run it: (N=5, W=1, R=5) and (N=5, W=5, R=1) both satisfy W+R>N (6>5 either way). Do they behave identically?

for n, w, r in [(5, 1, 5), (5, 5, 1)]:
    stale = sum(run_trial(n, w, r, num_writes=5, seed=s) for s in range(20000))
    print(n, w, r, "stale:", 100*stale/20000, "%")
# both print 0.0% -- same correctness guarantee.
# but time each one with w replicas acking a write vs r replicas
# answering a read, and the latency profile is opposite: cheap fast
# writes + expensive slow reads, or the reverse. W+R>N buys you
# the SAME safety at either end of that trade -- pick the end that
# matches whether your workload is write-heavy or read-heavy.

Both give 0% stale reads — same correctness, opposite cost. (W=1, R=5) is a write-optimized shape: one node has to ack before you move on, but every single read has to fan out to all five replicas. (W=5, R=1) is the mirror image: durable, slow writes and cheap, single-hop reads. This is the real design decision hiding behind "we use quorum" — the formula only tells you whether you're safe, not which side of the safety line to load the cost onto. Second stretch goal: add a sloppy=True flag to Cluster.write that, with some probability, redirects a write to a node outside the sampled W-subset (simulating hinted handoff to a stand-in node) — then watch even N=3, W=2, R=2 start producing stale reads, because the overlap guarantee only ever held against the fixed node set you enumerated over.

06 The senior/staff framing

What separates a strong answer from a weak one

  • Weak: "We use quorum reads and writes, so we're strongly consistent." Strong: "R+W>N guarantees a read's quorum overlaps the most recent acknowledged write's quorum — that's a regular register guarantee: you'll always see the latest acknowledged write, among live nodes, against the fixed replica set you enumerated over. It is not automatically linearizability. Two clients writing concurrently to overlapping quorums, with a coordinator crash mid-write, can still produce ordering surprises unless it's paired with per-key sequencing, read-repair, or a single serialization point. Quorum consistency gets you most of the strong-consistency guarantee for a fraction of the synchronous-replication-to-everyone latency — it isn't a free substitute for it."
  • The killer follow-up: "You run N=3, W=2, R=2. A write is acknowledged by nodes A and B; the coordinator crashes before read-repair reaches C. A client's quorum read happens to hit B and C — does it see the write? What if it hits A and C, and A is currently unreachable?" First case: yes, B is in both quorums, overlap holds, the read sees it. Second case: the math assumed R live responses — if A times out, the read has only gotten 1 of its required 2 responses, and now you're answering a completely different question the formula doesn't cover: what does the coordinator do with an under-quorate read? Block until timeout, degrade to whatever partial answer arrived, or fail loudly? R+W>N is silent on availability during partial failure — that's a second, separate design decision.
  • Know where quorum systems fail loudly and where they fail quietly: unreachable nodes make quorum reads/writes fail loudly (timeout, explicit error) — that's the easy case to reason about. Sloppy quorums and hinted handoff make them fail quietly: the operation still succeeds, but against a node set outside the one the R+W>N arithmetic was computed over, so the guarantee silently no longer applies to that particular write until hinted handoff replays it home.

Before you move on

A payments team is choosing a quorum shape for a 5-replica ledger cluster and is deciding between (N=5, W=3, R=3) and (N=5, W=1, R=5). Write traffic is heavy and latency-sensitive (customer-facing); reads are rare, batch reconciliation jobs that tolerate a few extra hundred milliseconds.

Which would you recommend, and why? Now suppose two of the five replicas sit in a distant region with 80ms extra round-trip latency — how does that change your recommendation for each of the two configurations, and does it change whether the R+W>N safety guarantee itself still holds?

08 Spaced recap

Resurfacing lesson 15 — CAP Theorem & PACELC

Lesson 15 established that you can't have Consistency and Availability during a network Partition, and — via PACELC — that even without a partition, you're still trading Latency against Consistency in the normal case. Today's lesson is the practical toolkit for the "C" side of both: strong consistency is the choice PACELC warns costs you latency even when nothing is broken, because every quorum write waits on the round-trip to its slowest live member; eventual consistency is the availability-favoring choice CAP allows once a partition actually splits the cluster. R+W>N is the dial that lets you sit anywhere between those two extremes while the network is healthy — but it doesn't repeal CAP. Split N replicas so neither side can reach a live quorum, and you're back to the exact same choice lesson 15 described: refuse requests to stay consistent, or serve them from a minority and stay available. The knob changes where you sit before the partition. It doesn't change what you're forced to pick during one.
