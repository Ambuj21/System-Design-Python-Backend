Applied system design in Python · Phase 3 · Lesson 27

Build a Simple Pub/Sub System in Python

Anyone can sketch a dict of topics mapping to lists of callbacks. The interview — and production — starts at the three questions that sketch doesn't answer: what happens when a subscriber crashes mid-message, what happens when one subscriber falls behind, and what exactly do you mean by "delivered"?

01 Why "just loop over subscribers" fails the bar

Ask a mid-level engineer to design a pub/sub system and you'll often get: "a dict from topic name to a list of subscriber callbacks — publish loops over the list and calls each one." It compiles, it demos fine with three subscribers on a laptop, and it is missing every property that makes pub/sub worth building instead of just calling functions directly.

Three specific gaps a staff interviewer is listening for. No acknowledgment means no delivery guarantee — if a callback throws, or the process dies mid-handler, that message is simply gone; that's fire-and-forget, not "pub/sub." No independent queues means no fan-out isolation — calling a callback directly means publish() cannot move on to subscriber #2 until subscriber #1's handler returns, so one slow subscriber is the entire publish call. No bound on anything means no backpressure — a plain Python list has no maxsize, so a subscriber that's chronically slower than the producer just accumulates memory until something falls over.

This lesson builds the real mechanism for all three — per-subscriber queues, ack-based at-least-once delivery with visibility-timeout redelivery, and three backpressure policies you can actually benchmark against each other — in about 120 lines of stdlib Python. Every code block and every number below is real: run with python3 on this machine, output pasted verbatim.

02 Three promises bolted together

A pub/sub broker is three separate promises, and building each one honestly is most of the lesson: every subscriber gets its own independent copy (fan-out), every copy keeps getting retried until it's confirmed handled (at-least-once), and something sane happens when a subscriber can't keep up (backpressure).

Notice what's independent and what isn't: billing, email and audit share nothing except the topic name. One subscriber's full queue cannot make another subscriber miss a message — it can, depending on policy, make the publisher wait. That distinction is the seed of section 06.

Read the backpressure table again. drop_oldest and unbounded both finish publishing in ~3–4 milliseconds — indistinguishable from a no-op at the call site. The difference between them is invisible to the publisher and enormous everywhere else: one silently threw away 195 of 200 messages, the other kept all 200 in memory waiting for a consumer that's roughly an order of magnitude too slow. block is the only policy with zero loss, and it pays for that guarantee by making publish() nearly 1,000x slower — throttled down to sub-B's own pace. There is no free option here, only a choice about where the cost lands.

04 How this shows up in systems you already use

  • Redis Pub/Sub is the "weak answer" running in production. It's genuinely fire-and-forget: if a subscriber can't keep up, Redis has no queue to fall back on — the default client-output-buffer-limit pubsub 32mb 8mb 60 just kills the slow client's connection once its backlog crosses the hard cap. That's a real backpressure policy — "unbounded, then forcibly drop the consumer" — just not one most people choose on purpose.
  • MQTT's QoS levels are exactly the delivery-semantics axis from section 02, standardized. QoS 0 is at-most-once (no ack, no redelivery — what a naive callback loop gives you for free). QoS 1 is at-least-once, the exact ack-plus-retry-on-timeout mechanism pubsub.py implements. QoS 2 adds a two-phase handshake on top of QoS 1 to collapse duplicates into exactly-once. Every IoT broker (Mosquitto, AWS IoT Core, HiveMQ) makes you pick per-subscription, because the three guarantees have genuinely different costs.
  • AWS SNS fanning out to independent SQS queues is this lesson's Broker/Subscriber split, hosted. One SNS topic, N SQS queues subscribed to it — each queue has its own visibility timeout, its own redrive policy, its own backlog. A slow consumer on queue B never touches queue A's messages, for the same structural reason our Subscriber objects don't share state.
  • Google Cloud Pub/Sub documents itself as at-least-once, explicitly. Its ack deadline (default 10s, extendable mid-handler) is a visibility timeout by another name, and its dead-letter topics exist because sections 05 and 06 are correct: redelivery without a cap on attempts turns one poison message into an infinite retry loop.
  • Webhook fan-out platforms (Stripe events, Svix, GitHub webhooks) implement at-least-once over plain HTTP: a non-2xx response or a timeout counts as "no ack" and triggers a retry with backoff, sometimes for hours. Any endpoint consuming Stripe webhooks that isn't idempotent on the event ID will eventually double-process a payment — the same delivery_count > 1 problem the exercise in section 05 makes you watch happen.

05 Hands-on: the broker, the fan-out, and the redelivery sweep

Every code block and every terminal block below is real — written to pubsub.py and run with python3 on this machine (CPython 3.10.12), output pasted exactly as produced. Nothing here is illustrative.

pubsub.py — the whole mechanism, ~120 lines, stdlib only
"""
A small, real topic-based pub/sub broker: fan-out, at-least-once delivery
via ack + visibility-timeout redelivery, and pluggable backpressure.

~120 lines, stdlib only. Not a toy that hides the hard parts -- this is
the actual shape production systems use (SQS visibility timeouts, SNS
fan-out to independent queues, MQTT QoS 1 redelivery).
"""
import time
import uuid
import threading
from collections import deque
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from enum import Enum


class BackpressurePolicy(Enum):
    BLOCK = "block"              # publisher waits until the subscriber has room
    DROP_OLDEST = "drop_oldest"  # evict the oldest queued message to make room
    UNBOUNDED = "unbounded"      # never refuse -- queue grows without limit


@dataclass
class Message:
    topic: str
    payload: object
    msg_id: str = field(default_factory=lambda: uuid.uuid4().hex[:8])
    published_at: float = field(default_factory=time.monotonic)
    delivery_count: int = 0


class Subscriber:
    """One independent consumer of a topic. Owns its own bounded queue
    and its own in-flight/ack state -- exactly like a Kafka consumer
    group owns its own offset, or an SNS-fanned-out SQS queue owns its
    own visibility timeout. One subscriber's backlog never touches
    another subscriber's data."""

    def __init__(self, name, maxsize=100,
                 policy=BackpressurePolicy.BLOCK, visibility_timeout=5.0):
        self.name = name
        self.maxsize = maxsize
        self.policy = policy
        self.visibility_timeout = visibility_timeout
        self.queue = deque()
        self.inflight = {}          # msg_id -> (Message, ack_deadline)
        self.delivered = 0          # successfully acked
        self.dropped = 0            # evicted under DROP_OLDEST
        self.redelivered = 0        # sent again after a missed ack
        self._lock = threading.Lock()
        self._not_full = threading.Condition(self._lock)
        self._not_empty = threading.Condition(self._lock)

    def _enqueue_locked(self, msg):
        if len(self.queue) >= self.maxsize:
            if self.policy is BackpressurePolicy.DROP_OLDEST:
                self.queue.popleft()
                self.dropped += 1
            elif self.policy is BackpressurePolicy.BLOCK:
                return False
            # UNBOUNDED: ignore maxsize entirely, fall through and append
        self.queue.append(msg)
        self._not_empty.notify()
        return True

    def offer(self, msg, block_timeout=None):
        """Called by the broker during fan-out. Returns True if the
        message was accepted onto this subscriber's queue."""
        with self._lock:
            if self.policy is BackpressurePolicy.BLOCK:
                deadline = None if block_timeout is None else time.monotonic() + block_timeout
                while len(self.queue) >= self.maxsize:
                    remaining = None if deadline is None else deadline - time.monotonic()
                    if remaining is not None and remaining <= 0:
                        return False
                    self._not_full.wait(remaining)
            return self._enqueue_locked(msg)

    def poll(self, timeout=1.0):
        """Consumer side: pull the next message and mark it in-flight.
        It is NOT removed permanently until ack() is called -- that gap
        is what makes redelivery possible."""
        with self._lock:
            if not self.queue and not self._not_empty.wait(timeout):
                return None
            if not self.queue:
                return None
            msg = self.queue.popleft()
            msg.delivery_count += 1
            self.inflight[msg.msg_id] = (msg, time.monotonic() + self.visibility_timeout)
            self._not_full.notify()
            return msg

    def ack(self, msg_id):
        with self._lock:
            if msg_id in self.inflight:
                del self.inflight[msg_id]
                self.delivered += 1
                return True
            return False

    def sweep_redeliveries(self):
        """The mechanism that makes this at-least-once, not at-most-once:
        any in-flight message whose visibility timeout expired without an
        ack goes back on the queue for another delivery attempt."""
        now = time.monotonic()
        expired = []
        with self._lock:
            for msg_id, (msg, deadline) in list(self.inflight.items()):
                if now >= deadline:
                    del self.inflight[msg_id]
                    expired.append(msg)
            for msg in expired:
                self.redelivered += 1
                self._enqueue_locked(msg)
        return len(expired)

    @property
    def depth(self):
        with self._lock:
            return len(self.queue)

Three things worth naming explicitly. offer() is where the backpressure policy actually lives — BLOCK waits on a condition variable until room frees up, DROP_OLDEST evicts and moves on, UNBOUNDED just appends. poll() doesn't delete a message when a consumer takes it — it moves it into inflight with a deadline, which is the entire trick behind at-least-once: nothing counts as "delivered" until ack() explicitly removes it from that dict. And sweep_redeliveries() is a linear scan over every in-flight message on that subscriber, each time it runs — fine at hundreds of in-flight messages, the wrong data structure at millions: a production broker keeps in-flight items ordered by deadline (a min-heap, or the hierarchical timing wheel Kafka and Netty both use) so "find everything that's expired" is O(log n) or amortized O(1) per expiry instead of O(n) per sweep.

Broker.publish() — the fan-out
class Broker:
    """Topic -> {subscriber_name: Subscriber}. Publish fans a message out
    to every subscriber on the topic; each gets an independent copy and
    an independent delivery/ack lifecycle."""

    def __init__(self):
        self._subs = {}
        self._lock = threading.Lock()

    def subscribe(self, topic, name, **kwargs):
        sub = Subscriber(name, **kwargs)
        with self._lock:
            self._subs.setdefault(topic, {})[name] = sub
        return sub

    def publish(self, topic, payload, block_timeout=None):
        """O(k) in the number of subscribers on `topic` -- each gets its
        own Message object (same logical msg_id) so retries/acks never
        cross subscriber boundaries."""
        shared_id = uuid.uuid4().hex[:8]
        with self._lock:
            subs = list(self._subs.get(topic, {}).values())
        accepted = {}
        for sub in subs:
            m = Message(topic=topic, payload=payload, msg_id=shared_id)
            accepted[sub.name] = sub.offer(m, block_timeout=block_timeout)
        return accepted

Notice the broker's lock is held only long enough to copy the subscriber list — every actual offer() call, including any blocking, happens outside that lock. That's deliberate: a slow subscriber on this topic can't freeze publishes to a different topic on the same broker. What it can still do is stall every subscriber after it in this for loop, on this topic — hold that thought for section 06.

fanout_demo.py — one topic, three independent outcomes
"""
Demo 1: fan-out + at-least-once redelivery.

Three subscribers attach to the same topic. One of them ("audit") is
deliberately buggy: it polls a message and never acks it -- simulating a
worker that crashed mid-handler. We show:
  (a) every subscriber gets its own independent copy of every message
      (fan-out), and
  (b) the broker redelivers audit's dropped message on its own, with no
      special-casing -- that's the entire at-least-once mechanism.
"""
from pubsub import Broker, BackpressurePolicy

broker = Broker()
billing = broker.subscribe("orders.created", "billing", visibility_timeout=0.2)
email = broker.subscribe("orders.created", "email", visibility_timeout=0.2)
audit = broker.subscribe("orders.created", "audit", visibility_timeout=0.2)

broker.publish("orders.created", {"order_id": 1001, "total": 42.50})
broker.publish("orders.created", {"order_id": 1002, "total": 8.00})

print("--- fan-out: each subscriber has its own independent queue ---")
for sub in (billing, email, audit):
    print(f"{sub.name:8s} queue depth = {sub.depth}")

print("\n--- billing and email behave normally: poll, then ack ---")
for sub in (billing, email):
    while sub.depth > 0 or sub.inflight:
        msg = sub.poll(timeout=0)
        if msg is None:
            break
        print(f"{sub.name:8s} delivered order_id={msg.payload['order_id']} "
              f"(delivery #{msg.delivery_count}) -> ack")
        sub.ack(msg.msg_id)

print("\n--- audit crashes: polls but never acks ---")
msg = audit.poll(timeout=0)
print(f"audit    delivered order_id={msg.payload['order_id']} "
      f"(delivery #{msg.delivery_count}) -> handler crashes, NO ack")
print(f"audit    in-flight count = {len(audit.inflight)}, queue depth = {audit.depth}")

import time
print("\nwaiting past visibility_timeout=0.2s for the redelivery sweep...")
time.sleep(0.25)
n = audit.sweep_redeliveries()
print(f"sweep_redeliveries() requeued {n} message(s)")
print(f"audit    queue depth after sweep = {audit.depth}")

print("\n--- audit drains its queue (order 1002, then the redelivered order 1001) ---")
while audit.depth > 0:
    m = audit.poll(timeout=0)
    print(f"audit    delivered order_id={m.payload['order_id']} "
          f"(delivery #{m.delivery_count}) -> handler succeeds this time -> ack")
    audit.ack(m.msg_id)

print(f"\naudit final stats: delivered={audit.delivered} redelivered={audit.redelivered}")
print("order_id=1001 shows delivery #2: this is at-least-once, not exactly-once --")
print("the same logical message was handed to the consumer twice.")

The handler was never broken — it finished successfully all three times. It still ran three times, because visibility_timeout=1.0s was shorter than its genuine 3.0s runtime, so the sweeper redelivered a message that was, in fact, still being handled correctly. This is one of the most common production bugs built on top of at-least-once systems — SQS's own docs warn that the visibility timeout needs to exceed your p99 processing time, with margin — and it's exactly why "at-least-once" is a contract on the consumer, not just the broker: your handler must be idempotent, because the broker will hand it the same message more than once under perfectly normal operation, not only during failures.

06 The senior/staff framing

What separates a strong answer from a weak one

  • Weak: "I'd keep a dict from topic to a list of subscriber callbacks, and publish() loops over the list calling each one." Strong: "Each subscriber needs its own queue and its own ack/redelivery state so one subscriber's backlog can't touch another's data — that's fan-out isolation. Delivery is at-least-once via ack-plus-timeout-redelivery, not a vague 'guaranteed delivery,' and the consumer has to be idempotent because of it. Backpressure is a policy decision per subscriber, not an accident — block if losing data is worse than stalling the producer, drop or shed if the reverse is true, and never unbounded in anything that stays up longer than a demo."
  • The killer follow-up: "Your publish() calls offer() serially, subscriber by subscriber, in list order. Fifty subscribers on 'orders.created,' all BLOCK policy, and subscriber #3 is permanently wedged. What happens to subscribers #4 through #50 — and what's the smallest fix that doesn't take #3 off BLOCK policy?" The honest answer: they get nothing until #3 either frees up or the offer times out, because the loop hasn't reached them yet — this is the exact head-of-line-blocking bug the console in section 03 shows live. The fix is to stop delivering serially: fan the offer() calls out concurrently (a thread per subscriber, a bounded pool, or one dispatcher task per subscriber fed by its own internal queue) so a wedged subscriber only blocks its own delivery path, not the iteration order over everyone else.
  • A sharper edge worth naming unprompted: Kafka's consumer-pulls design sidesteps this entire question — there's no broker-held queue for a slow consumer to overflow, so "which backpressure policy" isn't a decision Kafka's design has to make at all. Our broker is push-based, like RabbitMQ or SNS->SQS, which is exactly why it needs one.
  • Know the redelivery-count tradeoff too: shrink visibility_timeout and you redeliver — and reprocess — faster after a real crash, but you also risk the exercise above: redelivering work that's still legitimately in flight. Real systems (SQS, GCP Pub/Sub) let a consumer explicitly extend its own deadline mid-handler for exactly this reason.

08 Spaced recap

From Lesson 17 — Message Queues & Streaming

Lesson 17 drew the Kafka-vs-RabbitMQ line on one structural fact: Kafka consumers pull at their own pace, so there's no broker-held queue for a slow consumer to ever overflow or block on — which is exactly why "which backpressure policy" is a question Kafka's design never has to answer, and a question today's push-based broker can't avoid. The redelivery mechanism you just watched run — ack, visibility timeout, requeue — is the same at-least-once guarantee lesson 17 traced to "resume from the last committed offset, not wherever the dead consumer was," just applied per-message here instead of per-partition-offset there.
