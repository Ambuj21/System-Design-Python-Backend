System design fundamentals · Phase 2 · Lesson 22

Consensus Basics: Raft & Paxos

How a cluster of machines agrees on one leader and one totally ordered log — safely, even when messages get lost, nodes crash mid-write, and the network itself splits in half. The actual RPCs and arithmetic, not the folklore version.

01 Why this is where staff interviews actually dig in

Ask a senior engineer to explain consensus and you'll usually get: "a majority of nodes have to agree." That's not wrong — it's just the kind of answer that survives a phone screen and collapses under the first real follow-up: agree on what, through which messages, and what stops two different majorities from agreeing on two different things at once? The gap between "majority vote" and a working implementation is where Raft and Paxos actually live, and it is exactly the gap a staff-level interview is built to probe.

Three specific places candidates fall through:

— They describe leader election as "picking the healthiest or most available node." It isn't. Raft never ranks candidates by health at all; it only enforces that a candidate can't win unless its log is at least as complete as a majority of the electorate, and that any node casts at most one vote per term. Health never enters the algorithm — only two numbers do: term, and log completeness.

— They believe a write is safe the moment it's sent to a majority. Raft is stricter: a write becomes safe once the leader itself has observed acknowledgments from a majority — and, the part almost nobody says unprompted, replication count alone still isn't enough to commit an entry the leader inherited from a previous leader's term. Section 6 has the exact scenario.

— They conflate "the protocol tolerates a network partition" with "nothing interesting happens during one." What actually happens is more precise: one side keeps a leader that can still accept client requests but can never durably commit anything, while the other side elects a new leader and keeps making progress — and the two never conflict, not because either side detects the other, but because of quorum arithmetic alone. That arithmetic is the entire subject of this lesson.

02 The overlapping jury

Every consensus protocol — Raft, Paxos, Viewstamped Replication, all of them — reduces to one arithmetic fact about majorities. Forget the RPC names for a second and look at a jury instead.

That's the whole reason a network split can never produce two leaders — or two different committed values for the same log slot — at the same time. Raft and Paxos differ enormously in mechanism: how a leader gets picked, how proposals get numbered, how many message round-trips it costs. Neither one can violate this arithmetic, because neither one tries to. Section 3 shows the actual RPCs that enforce it.

Fault tolerance vs. cluster size

quorum = ⌊n/2⌋ + 1 · tolerates f = ⌊(n−1)/2⌋ node failures
n=3 · quorum 2f=1
n=4 · quorum 3f=1
n=5 · quorum 3f=2
n=6 · quorum 4f=2
n=7 · quorum 4f=3

Timing bounds that make it work

Raft paper §5.6: broadcastTime ≪ electionTimeout ≪ MTBF

broadcastTime — one AppendEntries round-trip. Roughly 0.5–20ms on a typical datacenter LAN.

electionTimeout — must be several multiples of broadcastTime, or a healthy leader's own heartbeat can race a false timeout. Raft's reference implementation used 150–300ms — the exact range this page's simulator uses.

MTBF — mean time between single-server failures, typically months. electionTimeout only needs to be tiny relative to this, so elections stay rare, not fast.

Read the n=4 and n=6 rows again. Adding a node to an already-odd cluster without going all the way to the next odd number buys nothing — you pay the extra replication bandwidth and the extra round-trip of a fourth or sixth voter while tolerating exactly as many failures as one node fewer would. This is the entire reason a well-run production Raft or Paxos cluster is sized 3, 5, or occasionally 7 — never 4 or 6.

04 How this shows up in systems you already use

  • etcd runs Raft under Kubernetes' entire control plane. Every kubectl apply, every scheduler decision, every ConfigMap read goes through an etcd Raft-replicated log with a single leader per term; that's also why a Kubernetes control plane needs 3 or 5 etcd members, never an even number, for exactly the fault-tolerance-table reason above.
  • HashiCorp Consul and Nomad both embed the hashicorp/raft library for leader election and their replicated KV store — one of the most widely deployed real-world Raft implementations outside etcd itself.
  • CockroachDB and TiKV (TiDB's storage layer) run Multi-Raft: every data shard ("range") is its own tiny independent Raft group with its own leader, so a single physical node is simultaneously the Raft leader for thousands of unrelated ranges. This is the pattern to name when an interviewer asks how you'd scale Raft past one log's throughput ceiling.
  • Kafka's KRaft mode (KIP-500, the default since Kafka 3.x/4.0) replaced the old ZooKeeper-based controller with a Raft-based metadata quorum — cluster membership and topic metadata are themselves now a Raft-replicated log.
  • ZooKeeper's Zab (Zookeeper Atomic Broadcast) predates Raft's popularity and solves the same problem with a leader-based, epoch-numbered protocol — the direct intellectual ancestor of a lot of what Raft later made legible. Google's original Chubby lock service ran classic multi-Paxos and underpinned GFS and BigTable's coordination.
  • Google Spanner runs Paxos per shard for replication, layered under TrueTime for external consistency — the consensus log gives ordering within a shard; TrueTime is what lets Spanner make cross-shard ordering claims without talking to every shard.
  • MongoDB replica sets (3.2+) use a Raft-derived election protocol for primary selection, even though the on-disk replication mechanics predate and differ from textbook Raft.

05 Hands-on: run a real election simulation

This is a discrete-event model of Raft leader election — randomized timeouts, independent per-link network delay, at-most-one-vote-per-term — not a toy that fakes the numbers. Run it yourself; the split-vote run below is a genuine emergent split, not a scripted one.

raft_election.py
import random

def simulate_election(n=5, seed=42, max_rounds=6):
    random.seed(seed)
    quorum = n // 2 + 1
    term = 0
    election_timeout = {i: random.randint(150, 300) for i in range(n)}
    print(f"cluster size n={n}, quorum={quorum}")
    print(f"election timeouts (ms): {election_timeout}")

    leader = None
    round_no = 0
    while leader is None and round_no < max_rounds:
        round_no += 1
        term += 1
        # per-round jitter models the randomized *re*-timeout Raft uses after a split vote
        jitter = {i: election_timeout[i] + random.randint(0, 60) * (round_no - 1) for i in range(n)}
        order = sorted(jitter, key=jitter.get)
        first = jitter[order[0]]
        # anyone whose timer fires within 5ms of the first also goes candidate
        candidates = [i for i in order if jitter[i] - first <= 5]

        print(f"\n--- term {term} (round {round_no}) ---")
        for c in candidates:
            print(f"  node {c} times out ({jitter[c]}ms) -> candidate, sends RequestVote to all")

        # each follower grants its one vote to whichever candidate's RequestVote
        # arrives first, given independent random network delay per link —
        # THIS is what makes a genuine split vote possible
        arrival = {}
        for voter in range(n):
            if voter in candidates:
                continue
            delays = {c: random.uniform(1, 15) for c in candidates}
            arrival[voter] = min(delays, key=delays.get)

        votes = {c: {c} for c in candidates}
        for voter, c in arrival.items():
            votes[c].add(voter)

        for c in candidates:
            print(f"  node {c} collects votes {sorted(votes[c])} -> {len(votes[c])}/{n}")
            if len(votes[c]) >= quorum:
                leader = c

        if leader is None:
            print(f"  no candidate reached quorum ({quorum}/{n}) -> SPLIT VOTE, all retry with fresh randomized timeout")

    if leader is not None:
        print(f"\n>>> node {leader} becomes LEADER for term {term}  (quorum {quorum}/{n})")
    else:
        print(f"\n>>> no leader elected within {max_rounds} rounds")
    return leader, term


def is_committed(match_index, n):
    """match_index[i] = highest log index node i has replicated.
    An index is committed once >= quorum nodes have replicated it."""
    quorum = n // 2 + 1
    highest = 0
    for idx in range(1, max(match_index) + 1):
        if sum(1 for m in match_index if m >= idx) >= quorum:
            highest = idx
    return highest


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("=" * 64)
    print("RUN 1 — clean election, single round")
    print("=" * 64)
    simulate_election(n=5, seed=7)

    print("\n" + "=" * 64)
    print("RUN 2 — split vote on term 1, resolved on retry (seed=11)")
    print("=" * 64)
    simulate_election(n=5, seed=11)

    print("\n" + "=" * 64)
    print("fault tolerance vs cluster size (why odd N)")
    print("=" * 64)
    print(f"{'n':>3} {'quorum':>7} {'tolerates f=floor((n-1)/2) failures':>36}")
    for n in range(3, 8):
        q = n // 2 + 1
        f = (n - 1) // 2
        print(f"{n:>3} {q:>7} {f:>36}")

    print("\n" + "=" * 64)
    print("commit-index advancement from matchIndex")
    print("=" * 64)
    for n, match_index in [(5, [4, 4, 3, 2, 1]), (5, [4, 4, 4, 2, 1]), (3, [5, 2, 2])]:
        c = is_committed(match_index, n)
        print(f"n={n} matchIndex={match_index} quorum={n//2+1} -> commitIndex advances to {c}")

n=3: split vote in  3/500 runs = 0.6%
n=4: split vote in 37/500 runs = 7.4%
n=5: split vote in  4/500 runs = 0.8%
n=6: split vote in 40/500 runs = 8.0%
n=7: split vote in  5/500 runs = 1.0%

Even clusters split roughly 8–10× more often than odd ones in this model. The reason is the same pigeonhole argument from section 2, run in reverse: with an even n, two simultaneous candidates can split the remaining followers exactly down the middle and tie; with an odd n, the remaining followers are an odd number too, so an exact tie between two candidates is structurally impossible — someone always edges ahead. Odd cluster sizes aren't just "more efficient per node" (the fault-tolerance table); they're also measurably more decisive.

06 The senior/staff framing

What separates a strong answer from a weak one

  • Weak: "Raft and Paxos work because a majority of nodes agree on a value." Strong: "A majority agreeing is necessary but not the full safety argument. Raft specifically forbids a leader from committing an entry purely by counting replicas if that entry was appended by an earlier leader in an earlier term — it can only directly commit entries from its own current term. Older entries get committed only indirectly, as a side effect of a same-term entry after them getting committed."
  • The killer follow-up: "Why isn't 'replicated on a majority' enough, all by itself, to call an entry committed forever?" Because leadership can still change after that point, and a new leader is only required to have a log that was up to date as of the vote that elected it — not one that's byte-identical to the old leader's. There's a known scenario (Raft paper, Figure 8) where an entry sits on a majority under one leader, a new leader gets elected without that specific entry needing to be part of what got it elected, and if a naive "committed once on N/2+1 nodes, ever" rule were used, that entry could later be silently overwritten — a real safety violation. Restricting direct commits to the leader's own current term closes that hole.
  • Also worth naming: leader election isn't a popularity contest. A candidate's RequestVote carries its last log index and term; a voter rejects it outright if its own log is more up to date — full stop, before term or anything else is even compared for that check. This is what stops an out-of-date node from ever becoming leader and silently rolling the cluster's data backward.
  • Know the terminology precisely: quorum is always relative to total configured membership, not to whichever nodes happen to be reachable right now — that single distinction is what section 3's partition tab exists to make visceral.

07 Check your understanding

Before you move on

A 7-node Raft cluster loses network connectivity between two groups — 4 nodes on one side, 3 on the other — for 90 seconds, then the link is restored. The leader happened to be on the 3-node side when the partition started.

Work through all three: (a) Can the 4-node side commit new writes during the 90 seconds, and why? (b) Can the 3-node side's leader commit new writes during that window? (c) If a client kept sending writes to that old leader the whole time and it kept appending them locally, what happens to those entries the moment the link comes back?

Answer this back to your mentor before starting the next lesson.

08 Spaced recap

From Lesson 16 — Consistency Models

Lesson 16 laid out the spectrum from linearizable down through sequential, causal, and eventual consistency, and the latency you buy back at each step down. Today's lesson is the machinery that makes the strongest point on that spectrum achievable at all: a Raft- or Paxos-replicated log, read through the leader (or via lease/read-index reads), is what a linearizable store is built out of underneath. When a system instead lets you read from followers for lower latency, it isn't using a weaker consensus protocol — it's using the exact same one, and deliberately choosing not to pay for the guarantee on the read path. Same tradeoff from Lesson 16, except now you've seen the specific commit-index and quorum mechanics that guarantee's price is actually paying for.
